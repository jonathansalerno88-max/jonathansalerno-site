Lens physics

From a bicycle light to the magnifying glass: a course on the optics of the eye and of lenses, built as a series of questions. Every section has an interactive figure to use, not just to look at.

The numbers in the figures are orders of magnitude calculated with simplified models, except where a source is cited. They are there to show how the quantities relate to one another, not to make diagnoses.

0 · Introduction

Lumens or candelas? Two bike lights

On r/askscience someone has to choose between two bicycle lights: the cheaper one claims more lumens (lm, total flux), the more expensive one more ‘cd/m²’. They ask which of the two numbers really says how much the light illuminates (the thread). To answer, you have to separate four quantities that get mixed up on the packaging, and that is the job of the whole first part of this page. Two more do not appear on the packaging but decide what you see: reflectance and contrast.

  • Lumens (lm, total flux): all the light that leaves the lamp, in every direction. It does not say where it goes.
  • Candelas (cd = lm/sr), that is lumens per steradian: how much light goes in one direction, for a headlight the centre of the beam. The same flux concentrated into a narrower beam gives more candelas.
  • Lux (lx = lm/m²), that is lumens per square metre: how much light reaches the road at a given distance, E = I / d². It is what you see in front of the wheel. Lights type-approved in Germany (StVZO) state exactly the lux at 10 m; the ANSI FL1 standard states the peak candelas and the beam distance, the distance at which the centre of the beam falls to 0.25 lux, roughly the light of the full moon.
  • Cd/m², that is nits: the luminance of the lens. It says how much the lamp dazzles someone looking straight at it, not how much it lights the road. On a spec sheet it is almost certainly a mistake for ‘candelas’, as one of the replies suspects.
  • Reflectance ρ (pure number, 0 to 1): the fraction of light a surface sends back. For a matte surface L = ρ · E / π, that is from the lux (lx = lm/m²) that arrive to the nits (nit = cd/m²) you see. Under the same sun asphalt (ρ ≈ 0.2) and fresh snow (ρ ≈ 0.85) receive the same lux, but snow sends back about four times the nits, and about four times the trolands (Td = cd/m² × mm²) reach the retina. A little less in practice, because the pupil narrows.
  • Contrast (L white / L black): how far white stands out from black. When two lights fall on the same surface, like the projector and the room on the screen, ρ multiplies both and cancels out:
    contrast ≈ 1 + Eprojector / Eroom
    Example: a 3,000-lumen (lm, total flux) projector on a 100″ screen (about 2.8 m²) brings about 1,100 lux. In the dark, with about 1 lux from the room, only the projector limits the contrast; in a living room by day, with about 500 lux, it is about 3:1 and the picture looks washed out.

Take-home: what to look at when you buy

Phone

  • Sustained full-screen nits (nit = cd/m²): at least 1,000 to read it in the sun. Ignore the HDR (High Dynamic Range) peak, which is measured on a small window of the screen.
  • A good anti-reflective coating: for the same nits it reads better in the sun.

Projector

  • ANSI (American National Standards Institute) lumens (lm, total flux), and only those. Be wary of ‘LED lumens’, ‘light-source lumens’ or numbers with no acronym, which are often inflated three or four times.
  • Native contrast, which counts in the dark, that is where a projector does best.

The nits on the screen are then up to you, through three choices:

  • How dark you make the room. It is the strongest lever: in the dark about 48 nits are enough, in daylight they are never enough.
  • How big you make the picture. The same lumens over twice the area give half.
  • Which screen you use. White for more nits in the dark, ALR (Ambient Light Rejecting) if you cannot darken the room.

Why a more reflective screen does not save the contrast. The screen sends back the projector's light and the room's light with the same reflectance ρ, so ρ cancels out:

contrast ≈ (ρ·Eprojector + ρ·Eroom) / (ρ·Eroom) = 1 + Eprojector / Eroom

What counts is the ratio between the lux (lx = lm/m²) the projector brings and the lux the room brings. You change it with more lumens, a smaller picture or a darker room; with the screen, only if it is ALR, because it sends back the projector's light more than the light from the windows.

In the thread's example, light A has 1,000 lumens (lm, total flux) in a 40° beam and light B 400 lumens in a 10° beam. B has fewer lumens but about six times the candelas (cd = lm/sr) of A: at 10 m it puts about 170 lux (lx = lm/m²) on the road against 26, and reaches two and a half times as far. A, on the other hand, lights a wider strip of road. To see far ahead, what counts is the candelas, or the lux at 10 m; to see to the sides, the lumens spread over a wide beam.

Now it's your turn. You have to light your lane where you need to see: at the stopping distance, that is 1 s of reaction plus braking. At 22 km/h that is about 10 m. There you need at least 60 lux (lx = lm/m²) over 3.5 m of width, without blinding the cyclist coming the other way or the pedestrian walking towards you on the pavement. Six faces judge the light you have chosen: light on the road, width, cyclist, pedestrian, price and battery life.

Try this. Goal: see as far as the stopping distance without dazzling the pedestrian, the cyclist or anyone coming the other way.
  1. Choose the speed: the game works out how far you need to see.
  2. Adjust lumens (lm, total flux) and beam width until light on the road and width turn green.
  3. Check the pedestrian and the cyclist: if they are sad, narrow the beam or switch on the cut-off.
  4. Look at price and battery life, and pick your compromise.

So which light should you buy? If you ride where there are other people (towns, cycle paths, roads): a light with a cut-off beam, that is, StVZO-approved. It puts the light on the road and not in the eyes of oncoming people, and for the same lumens (lm, total flux) it lights more. Between two such lights, compare the stated lux at 10 m (lx = lm/m²): higher means you see better. If you ride fast at night off-road, alone, a round beam is fine too: there you look at the candelas (cd = lm/sr) for distance and the lumens for the sides, and two lights, one narrow and one wide, work best. Lumens alone are never enough to choose, and the ‘cd/m²’ in the original question is almost certainly a mistake for ‘candelas’.

1 · Luminance, illuminance and glare

What lumens, candelas, nits, lux and trolands measure

The quantities of light are not alternatives to one another: they describe the same light at different points.

  • Lumen (luminous flux, lm): how much light the source emits in total.
  • Candela (luminous intensity, cd = lm/sr): how much it emits in one direction, per unit solid angle.
  • Nit (nit = cd/m²), that is candelas per square metre (luminance): how concentrated the emission is, the intensity divided by the apparent area of the source. The sun is around 10⁹ nits, a smartphone 1,000–2,000.
  • Lux (lx = lm/m²), that is lumens per square metre (illuminance): how much light arrives on a surface. Full sun gives about 100,000 lux.
  • Troland (retinal illuminance, Td = cd/m² × mm²): luminance in nits × pupil area in mm².

Luminance belongs to whatever emits or reflects: the sun, the road. What reaches the eye is not a luminance: it is an illuminance, in lux (lx = lm/m²), and on the retina a retinal illuminance, in trolands (Td = cd/m² × mm²). In the figure each unit has its own colour, the same wherever it appears.

Every source, direct or reflected, always has a luminance and always produces an illuminance on what it strikes. An illuminated matt surface in turn becomes a source:

L ≈ ρ · E / π

where ρ is the reflectance. Light asphalt (ρ ≈ 0.2) under 10,000 lux (lx = lm/m²) has about 600 nits (nit = cd/m²); fresh snow (ρ ≈ 0.85) about four times as much.

For an extended source, the illuminance on the retina depends only on the luminance and the pupil, not on the distance: the solid angle cancels out.

Eretina ∝ L · Apupil / f²

Hence the distinction that matters: glare depends on the illuminance the source produces at the eye, divided roughly by the square of the angle from the line of sight; damage to the retina depends on the luminance of what you stare at and on time.

Try this. Goal: find out which quantity changes when the glare disappears.
  1. Put the traffic light in front of the sun: the sun's lumens (lm, total flux), candelas (cd = lm/sr) and nits (nit = cd/m²) do not change.
  2. Look at the illuminance at the eye, in lux (lx = lm/m²): it collapses. That is what decides glare.
  3. Switch on the snow: the road's luminance rises about fourfold, but stays below the 10⁴ nit (nit = cd/m²) safety threshold. It dazzles, it does not damage.

2 · How the retina gets damaged

Photochemical dose, thermal threshold, and the eclipse trap

There are two distinct mechanisms.

  • Photochemical (blue light): what counts is the dose, that is irradiance × time on the same point of the retina. It accumulates: a brief intense exposure is equivalent to a long weak one. It is the mechanism of solar retinopathy. The ICNIRP/ACGIH exposure limit is a blue-light-weighted radiance, 100 J/(cm²·sr) over the course of a day; staring at the sun you exceed it in a matter of seconds.
  • Thermal: what counts is the instantaneous power, because heat dissipates. Either you exceed the threshold at once or nothing happens. It is the risk of lasers, which enter collimated and are concentrated into a spot of about 0.02 mm.

For white light there is an ICNIRP rule of thumb: below 10⁴ cd/m² of luminance not even a detailed risk assessment is needed. The real threshold, however, is expressed in radiance weighted on the blue spectrum, not in nits (nit = cd/m²).

The eclipse. The visible part of the sun has the same luminance as the full sun; only the illuminance at the eye falls. The damage is done by luminance, but the alarm bell is rung by illuminance: without discomfort the reflex to look away does not kick in, the pupil dilates, and the retina has no pain receptors. You stare for a long time at a source that burns like the full sun.

Try this. Goal: find out when eye drops that constrict the pupil protect the retina.
  1. Sun, tick the eye drops: the pupil was already about 2 mm, and the dose drops by only a fifth.
  2. Eclipse at 90%, tick the eye drops: they stop the pupil dilating to 4 mm and divide the dose by five.
  3. Move the time to 2 minutes: the limit is still exceeded tens of times over, because every point of the crescent is as bright as the full sun.
  4. Laser: the eye drops change nothing, because the 1 mm beam enters any pupil whole.

The eye drops do not change the lux (lx = lm/m²) at the eye: they change the pupil area, and so the trolands (Td = cd/m² × mm²). Which eye drops constrict the pupil, and which muscle they act on, is drawn in section 3.

The screen. The risk to the retina is negligible, with or without a filter. The real effect is on the body clock: reading for about 4 hours an evening for 5 evenings on a tablet, compared with a paper book, Chang and colleagues measured evening melatonin reduced by 55%, onset of secretion delayed by more than 1.5 hours, falling asleep slower by about 10 minutes and lower alertness in the morning. The effect that matters is the shift of the clock, which with a fixed alarm becomes social jet lag. The conditions of the study (maximum brightness, dimly lit room) have been criticised as exaggerated, and the authors replied; the question is open.

On blue-light filters, the 2023 Cochrane review concludes that they probably do not reduce computer-related eye strain and that the effect on sleep is uncertain, with very low-certainty evidence.

3 · The eye: model, refractive errors, accommodation, drugs

The reduced eye

For calculations the eye is treated as a single refracting surface, with a power of about 60 dioptres at rest and an interior of index 4/3: this is the reduced eye. “Reduced” compared with the real eye, which has at least four refracting surfaces (the two faces of the cornea and the two of the lens; six in Gullstrand's schematic eye, which splits the lens into cortex and nucleus). The model reduces them to one: instead of two principal points and two nodal points there is one of each, and the sums take a single formula. That surface is not the real cornea: it lies about 1.6 mm behind it, where the principal planes of the eye fall. Measured from the cornea, the points that matter are:

  • anterior focal point F: about 15 mm in front of the cornea (16.7 mm in front of the surface, 1/60 of a metre);
  • nodal point N′: about 7 mm behind the cornea, at the posterior pole of the lens. It is the centre of curvature of the surface, so a ray aimed there crosses it without bending;
  • posterior focal point F′: about 24 mm behind the cornea, that is on the retina of an emmetropic eye 24 mm long.

The N′–retina distance, about 16.7 mm, sets the size of images: one degree of visual field takes up about 0.29 mm of retina, and the sun, when stared at, forms a tiny disc of about 0.15 mm.

Where the focal point falls relative to the retina

The distance that decides everything is the one between the posterior nodal point N′, on the posterior face of the lens, and the focal point F′. It sets the size of the image on the retina and, compared with the fixed N′–retina distance (about 16.7 mm), tells whether the relaxed eye focuses at infinity.

  • Emmetropic: at rest F′ falls on the retina.
  • Myopic: F′ falls in front. Accommodating does not help, because it adds power.
  • Hyperopic: F′ falls behind. When young it compensates by accommodating, with effort.
  • Astigmatic: two powers, two focal points, one per meridian. By accommodating you bring one into focus at a time.

Rule of thumb: about 3 dioptres per millimetre. An eye 1 mm longer is myopic by about −3 D; for the same reason an error of 1 mm in biometry before cataract surgery becomes about 3 D of error in the intraocular lens.

For a near object it is not F′ that must lie on the retina, but the image. The ciliary muscle contracts, the zonule (the suspensory ligaments) slackens, the lens becomes rounder, the power rises and F′ moves forward in front of the retina. The relation is the lens equation, with the distances measured from N′:

1/f = 1/d₀ + 1/dᵢ    m = −dᵢ / d₀

Try this. Goal: see how refractive errors, drugs and age move the focus relative to the retina.
  1. Emmetropic, object at infinity, muscle relaxed: sharp. Raise the accommodation: infinity goes out of focus.
  2. Hyperopia + cycloplegic: the accommodation that was compensating disappears. That is why cyclopentolate is used to test children's eyes.
  3. Emmetropic + pilocarpine: accommodation locked too high, but the small pupil limits the blur.
  4. Tick “presbyopia”, object at about 40 cm (slider all the way right), muscle at 2.5 D: the lens stops at 1 D and reading stays blurred.
  5. Untick “presbyopia”, muscle at 1.5 D (early presbyopia), tick the eye drops that constrict only the pupil: the blur falls from about 14 to about 6 arcminutes. More depth of field, not more accommodation.

Drugs act on the ciliary muscle, which is smooth and controlled by the parasympathetic system through muscarinic receptors. This is why the opposite of pilocarpine is not muscle relaxants, which act on skeletal muscle, but cycloplegics (atropine, cyclopentolate, tropicamide).

The iris muscles and the ciliary muscle

The pupil is set by two smooth muscles that pull in opposite directions. The sphincter, a ring around the edge of the pupil, constricts it: it is controlled by the parasympathetic system, along cranial nerve III and the ciliary ganglion, with acetylcholine on M3 muscarinic receptors, the same as the ciliary muscle. The dilator, arranged radially, widens it: it is controlled by the sympathetic system, with three neurons (hypothalamus, ciliospinal centre, superior cervical ganglion) and noradrenaline on α1 receptors. Every eye drop that changes the pupil acts on one of the two. The ciliary muscle, the one for accommodation, lies on the parasympathetic pathway: in fact, it takes almost all the fibres of the ciliary ganglion, about 97%, against 3% for the sphincter. The sympathetic system sends it only a small β2 branch that relaxes it slightly. This is why complete relaxation is obtained by blocking M3 (cycloplegia), not by stimulating the sympathetic system.

Try this. Goal: recognise which muscle each drug and each lesion acts on.
  1. Dim light, pilocarpine and then phentolamine: both constrict the pupil, but the first pulls the sphincter, the second takes strength from the dilator.
  2. Horner: the difference between the two eyes shows best in the dark. Cranial nerve III: it shows best in the light.
  3. Look near: ciliary muscle contracted, zonule slack, lens rounded, pupil smaller. This is the near triad.
  4. Atropine: the ciliary muscle stays flat even at near. Pilocarpine: it contracts even when looking far.

Constricting the pupil without accommodating

The eye drops that only constrict the pupil do not change the optics of the eye: cornea and lens stay the same, with their defects. They change which part of the optics is used. The iris stops the peripheral rays, which are the ones that err the most, so the spot a point leaves on the retina gets smaller. By how much depends on the defect:

  • Defocus, that is the focal point in the wrong place, as in the presbyope reading: the small pupil does not move the focal point, it narrows the cone of rays. The spot follows the diameter: half the pupil, half the spot. This is the pinhole effect.
  • Spherical aberration: the peripheral rays are bent too much. The spot grows roughly with the cube of the diameter: half the pupil, about an eighth of the spot.
  • Chromatic aberration: blue comes to a focus before red, with about 2 D of difference across the visible. Like defocus, it follows the diameter.

The price is light, which follows the area of the pupil, and below about 2 mm diffraction, which spreads light out when it passes through a small hole. It is the same rule as the aperture of a camera. And the eye already knows it: when looking near it constricts the pupil together with accommodation and convergence. The eye drops reproduce only the miosis of the near triad, the only piece the presbyope can still buy.

How small must the spot be? There are two limits. The first is diffraction: even with perfect optics a point becomes a tiny disc, whose core is, at half intensity, about 9/D micrometres wide with the pupil D in millimetres: about 1.3 µm at 7 mm, 3 µm at 3 mm, 6 µm at 1.5 mm. The second is the cones: in the fovea there is one about every 2.5 µm, and a spot smaller than a cone carries no extra detail, because the retina cannot tell it apart. With a pupil of 2.5–3 mm the healthy eye is already close to this limit.

Try this. Goal: find out when constricting the pupil helps and when it makes things worse.
  1. Spherical aberration, pupil from 7 to 3 mm: the outer rays end up against the iris and the spot almost vanishes.
  2. Presbyope reading: the spot shrinks in proportion to the diameter, but the focus stays behind the retina.
  3. Emmetropic: as you constrict, the spot grows because of diffraction and the light falls.
  4. Chromatic aberration: yellow and green at the centre, orange around them, blue and violet further out.
  5. Switch to “real cones”: the blue-violet halo almost vanishes, because the centre of the fovea has no S cones.

The panels below the rays are at true scale: each small circle is a cone that lights up like a pixel. The angle covered by one cone is its width divided by the focal length N′F′, from the posterior nodal point to the retina: 2.5 µm / 16.7 mm ≈ 0.5 arcminutes.

4 · Spectacle lenses

Menisci, and why they are not biconvex

Whether a lens is converging or diverging is decided by where it is thicker, not by the shape of its faces: thicker at the centre it converges, thicker at the edges it diverges. Spectacles are almost always menisci, with both faces curved the same way (from μηνίσκος, little moon).

The meniscus shape is not there to counter spherical aberration, which with a beam as wide as the pupil is negligible. It is there to counter oblique astigmatism: the eye rotates behind the lens, and a lens crossed at a slant behaves as if it had two powers. By curving it around the centre of rotation of the eye, a sideways gaze crosses it almost perpendicularly.

5 · Parallax: nodal point or entrance pupil

What to rotate around to avoid errors

Among panoramic photographers the saying is to rotate the camera around the "nodal point". It is a historical inaccuracy: parallax depends only on the centre of perspective, that is the point from which the lines of sight towards the objects start, and that point is the entrance pupil, the image of the aperture stop seen through the front lenses. With a single lens and the stop right against it the two points coincide, and that is where the rule comes from; in a compound lens they separate, by centimetres even in wide-angle lenses.

The posterior nodal point remains important for another reason: together with the focal plane it defines the focal length.

The real eye rotates around its centre of rotation, about 13.5 mm behind the cornea: the "wrong" point for panoramic photography. The entrance pupil is at about 3 mm, N′ at about 7 mm. As the gaze moves, near objects slide a little relative to distant ones: this is ocular parallax, which the brain compensates for.

6 · Focal length and pupil in animals

Detail, field and light cannot be had all at once

With the same cones, lengthening N′F′ enlarges the image: the same detail covers more photoreceptors, but the field covered by the fovea narrows, as with a telephoto lens. If the pupil does not grow, the image also gets darker, because the brightness on the retina goes with the inverse square of the f-number, that is the focal length divided by the diameter of the pupil (as in photography: f/2 is bright, f/8 is dark).

  • Birds of prey: long focal length, large and detailed image, but a lot of light is needed.
  • Cat: short focal length and huge pupil, a lot of light but a small image.
  • Owl: both, with a tubular eye. The price is a narrow field and eyes that do not rotate in the orbit: the head rotates instead.

Why don't we have a raptor's focal length?

With a longer N′F′ the image on the retina would be larger and each cone would cover a smaller angle: more detail. But it would not pay off for us, for four reasons.

  1. Diffraction does not improve. The diffraction limit is an angle, 1.22·λ/D, and depends only on the pupil diameter, not on the focal length. With our daytime pupil of 2–3 mm it is already about 0.8–1.1 arcminutes, roughly what our cones can resolve (about 1 arcminute). Lengthening the focal length without widening the pupil would put more cones under an image already blurred by diffraction: no extra detail. A raptor lengthens the focal length and widens the pupil too.
  2. Less light. For the same pupil, retinal brightness goes as (D/f)²: with a focal length 1.5 times longer the image is about 2.3 times darker. A raptor hunts in full daylight; we need to see at dusk as well.
  3. A narrower field. The fovea has a fixed width on the retina: with a longer focal length it covers a smaller angle, and to see a scene sharply we would have to move our eyes and head more.
  4. A bigger eye. More focal length means a longer eyeball, so larger orbits and skull and more weight to move. Raptors have eyes as large as ours, or larger, in a much smaller head.

Our eye is a generalist's compromise: by day it is already close to the diffraction limit, at dusk it still gets enough light, and it covers a wide field.

7 · The thin lens

The classic ray diagram

Three rays are enough: the one parallel to the axis, which after the lens passes through F′; the one through the centre, which is not deviated; the one through F, which comes out parallel. Where they cross is the tip of the image. If after the lens they diverge, you extend them backwards and find a virtual image.

LensObjectImage
Convergingbeyond 2Freal, inverted, reduced, between F′ and 2F′
Convergingat 2Freal, inverted, same size (m = −1)
Convergingbetween F and 2Freal, inverted, magnified, beyond 2F′
Convergingat Fat infinity
Converginginside Fvirtual, upright, magnified
Diverginganywherevirtual, upright, reduced

The magnifying glass is a converging lens used with the object inside the focal point. It magnifies about 25 cm / f. Reading glasses do the same thing, but are ten times weaker; glasses for myopia are diverging and make things smaller.

8 · Real or virtual image

The sheet-of-paper test

An image is real when the light really passes through that point: a sheet of paper placed there catches it, and anyone in the room sees it, because the paper scatters the light in all directions. It is virtual when the rays diverge as if they came from a point they have never passed through: only someone who puts their eye in their path sees it, and it never appears on a sheet of paper.

With a single lens or mirror, inverted means real and upright means virtual. With more elements the rule no longer holds: in the astronomical telescope the final image is inverted but virtual.

Try this at home. Goal: find the real image of a lens with a sheet of paper.
  1. Stand facing a window with a sheet of white paper.
  2. Hold a magnifying glass in front of the paper and slowly move it away.
  3. At a few centimetres the window appears, small and upside down: it is a real image.
  4. Try again with glasses: with glasses for myopia it never appears; with reading glasses it does, but 30 cm–1 m from the lens.

Everyday virtual images: the bathroom mirror, the car's side mirror, the door peephole, the bottom of the swimming pool that looks closer. Real images: the projector screen, the camera sensor, the retina, the dot of sunlight concentrated by a lens.
