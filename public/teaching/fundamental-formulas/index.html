1 · The Starling equation

What it is and why it is useful

What it is. The Starling equation describes the role of hydrostatic and oncotic forces (the Starling forces) in the movement of fluid across the capillary wall. It grew out of a hypothesis put forward in 1896 by the English physiologist Ernest Starling.

Why it is useful. To predict whether, in a tissue, fluid leaves the capillary or returns to it. So to understand why oedema appears, or why a tissue dries out, and which of the forces has changed.

The historical context

  • Who he was. Ernest Starling (1866–1927), professor of physiology at University College London from 1899 to 1923. He worked all his life with his brother-in-law William Bayliss. We remember him for three things: the equation that bears his name (in fact written by Kedem and Katchalsky half a century later, with Staverman's coefficient σ), the word “hormone”, born with the discovery of secretin, and the Frank–Starling law of the heart.
  • The experiment (1896). He asks how tissue fluid gets back into the blood. He injects saline into a dog's leg: the blood absorbs it. Plasma, instead, stays there.
  • The idea. Plasma proteins pull water in and balance the pressure pushing it out.
Read more: the dog experiment, animated, and the full story

Who he was. Ernest Henry Starling (London 1866 – 1927) entered Guy's Hospital medical school at 16. In 1892 he went to Breslau to work with Rudolf Heidenhain, and from 1899 he was professor of physiology at University College London.

What the question was. How is lymph formed? For Carl Ludwig it was plasma filtered across the capillary wall, pushed by blood pressure.

Who Heidenhain was. Rudolf Heidenhain (1834–1897), professor of physiology at Breslau from 1859, when he was 25. He was the great student of secretion: he showed that saliva and gastric juice are active products of the glands and that the cells of the renal tubules secrete (he made it visible with a dye, indigo carmine). He devised the “gastric pouch” later improved by Pavlov, who worked in his laboratory. On glands and kidney he was right: the mistake was to extend the same idea to capillaries. His son Martin (1864–1949), professor of anatomy at Tübingen, is the histologist of Heidenhain's iron haematoxylin.

Heidenhain's idea. Lymph does not filter passively: the capillary wall actively produces it, as a gland produces saliva. He thought so because of two clues that filtration alone did not explain:

  • pressure down, lymph up: in some experiments arterial pressure collapsed and thoracic duct lymph increased;
  • the “lymphagogues”: certain substances increased lymph as a diuretic increases urine. A first class (peptone, extracts of leech and crayfish), a second (strong solutions of salts, sugar, urea).

Heidenhain's conclusion: physics is not enough, cells that secrete are needed.

Starling's answer. Heidenhain was looking at the wrong pressure. The same data are explained by just two physical variables:

  • the pressure in the capillaries, not in the arteries. If the inferior vena cava is tied, arterial pressure falls, but in the liver capillaries, upstream of the obstacle, it rises: and it is from the liver that the extra lymph comes. This is Pc.
  • the permeability of the wall. The first-class lymphagogues make the wall more permeable, above all in the liver. This is Kf (and σ). Today we would say: substances that release histamine and other mediators, the same ones as in anaphylaxis (histamine was discovered later; that peptone releases it was shown in the 1930s).
  • capillary pressure again. The second-class lymphagogues act by osmosis: they draw water from the tissues into the blood, volume rises and so does capillary pressure. Pc again. Today we would say: osmotic agents such as mannitol or hypertonic saline, used precisely to draw water out of the tissues, cells included (for example in cerebral oedema).

No secretion, then: capillary pressure and permeability, that is, two of the terms of the equation.

The 1896 experiment. It remained to explain how fluid gets back. Starling injected saline into the tissues of a dog's hind leg and saw that it passed directly into the blood; plasma injected in the same way stayed where it was. His explanation: the wall lets salts and water through but holds proteins back, and the plasma proteins exert an osmotic pressure that is small, yet of the same order of magnitude as the pressure in the capillaries. Two opposing forces that nearly balance: this is the idea that still bears his name, in a fourteen-page paper.

The equation is not his. Starling stated the hypothesis in words. Direct measurements came later (Landis in 1927, Pappenheimer and Soto-Rivera in 1948), and today's form, with the coefficient σ, was born more than half a century later from the work of Staverman and of Kedem and Katchalsky.

The same Starling. With his brother-in-law William Bayliss he discovered secretin (1902) and introduced the word “hormone” (1905); then he studied the heart as a pump. The Frank–Starling law in the animation below is his.

Diffusion and filtration

Water crosses the capillary wall in two ways:

  • by diffusion: molecules are exchanged in both directions, with no net shift of volume;
  • by filtration: a bulk flow driven by a pressure difference.

The Starling equation deals only with filtration. The orders of magnitude:

ProcessPer minutePer day
filtration in the glomerular capillaries125 mLabout 180 L
filtration in all the other capillaries of the bodyabout 20 mLabout 29 L
diffusion of water across all the capillariesabout 56 Labout 80,000 L

Check: 125 mL/min × 1440 min = 180 L; 20 mL/min × 1440 min = 28.8 L; 80,000 L / 1440 min ≈ 56 L/min. Diffusion exchanges thousands of times more water than filtration, but shifts no volume: it is filtration that decides whether a tissue swells.

The equation

Jv = Kf · [(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)]

  • [(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)] is the net driving force, in mmHg;
  • Kf is the proportionality constant, which turns the push into a flow;
  • Jv is the net flow of fluid between capillary and interstitium.

Sign convention: a force pushing out of the capillary is positive, one pulling in is negative. If Jv is positive, fluid tends to leave (filtration); if it is negative, it tends to return (absorption). So Pc pushes out and Pi opposes it; πc, the oncotic pressure of plasma proteins (mainly albumin), pulls in and πi pulls out.

The six variables (and the result)

[M] mass, [L] length, [T] time. Pressure = force / area = [M L T⁻²] / [L²]. Check: Kf × pressure = [M⁻¹ L⁴ T] × [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] = [L³ T⁻¹] = Jv ✓. Lp = Kf / area = [M⁻¹ L² T].

The first four are the forces: together they give the net driving force, in mmHg. σ is not a force: it multiplies only the oncotic difference and says how much of it really counts for that wall. Kf is the constant of proportionality that turns force into flow: Jv = Kf × net driving force, in mL/min. A high Kf means a capillary very permeable to water (glomerulus); a low Kf, a poorly permeable one (brain).

Kf is the product of two factors: the capillary surface area available (cm²) and the hydraulic conductance of the wall (Lp, how much fluid crosses each cm² of wall per mmHg: mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹·cm⁻²). So Kf = surface area × Lp. The mmHg is a pressure: 1 mmHg ≈ 133.3 Pa (Pa = N/m²).

In the figure of the old man with swollen ankles the numbers are the net driving force in mmHg, not the flow: to get Jv you would multiply them by that bed's Kf, which the model does not compute.

The reflection coefficient

σ is a correction factor, from 0 to 1. The oncotic difference matters because the wall holds proteins back and lets water and small solutes through (ultrafiltration). But many capillaries leak a little albumin, with two consequences:

  • πi is higher than it would be with a perfect wall;
  • not all the plasma protein is effective in holding water, so the effective πc is lower than the measured one.

Both effects reduce the weight of the oncotic difference, and σ corrects for it:

  • glomerular capillaries: σ close to 1, because normally no protein crosses into the filtrate;
  • hepatic sinusoids: low σ, because they are very permeable to proteins. This is useful: albumin is made by hepatocytes and so enters the blood easily.

Approximate values along the capillary

Point of the capillaryPcPiσπcσπiNet driving force
arteriolar end+35−2+25+0.1+12
venular end+15−2+25+3−5

All in mmHg (1 mmHg ≈ 133.3 Pa; Pa = N/m²), dimensions [M L⁻¹ T⁻²].

  1. Arteriolar end. (35 − (−2)) − (25 − 0.1) = 37 − 24.9 ≈ +12 mmHg: fluid leaves.
  2. Venular end. (15 − (−2)) − (25 − 3) = 17 − 22 = −5 mmHg: in the classic model, fluid returns.
  3. Mean along the capillary. If the force falls linearly, the mean is (12 − 5) / 2 = +3.5 mmHg: slightly more fluid leaves than returns, and the lymphatics drain the excess.

Oedema appears when the net force stays positive at the venular end too and the filtrate exceeds what the lymphatics can drain.

Clinical usefulness

The equation is very useful for understanding what happens at the capillary, much less for calculations on a patient: the six variables cannot all be measured together at the bedside.

Indicative values, for teaching. We now know that in many tissues venous reabsorption is modest and that the endothelial glycocalyx shapes the oncotic forces: the logic of the equation stays the same.

2 · Fick's law: diffusion

What it is and why it is useful

What it is. Fick's law (1855) says how much of a substance crosses by diffusion, that is, by the net movement of molecules from where they are more concentrated to where they are less, by thermal agitation alone. It is the first of the three routes on the transporters page, the one through the lipids.

Why it is useful. To predict how fast a substance crosses a membrane. So to understand gas exchange in the lung, and why it worsens when the membrane thickens or the surface shrinks.

It comes in two forms: the general one, valid for any dissolved substance, and the one modified for gases, which is the one of pulmonary gas exchange.

A · The general form

J = −D · ∂C / ∂x

The flux is proportional to how steeply concentration changes across space (the gradient ∂C/∂x). The minus sign only says that the flux goes towards the lower concentration. Here J is a flux per unit area.

SymbolWhat it isUnitDimensions
Jrate of transfer per unit area (the result)mol·cm⁻²·s⁻¹[N L⁻² T⁻¹]
Ddiffusivity (diffusion coefficient): depends on the molecule, the medium and the temperaturecm²·s⁻¹[L² T⁻¹]
Cconcentration of the diffusing substancemol·cm⁻³[N L⁻³]
xspace coordinate, measured normal to the surfacecm[L]

[N] amount of substance, [L] length, [T] time. Check: [L² T⁻¹] × [N L⁻³] / [L] = [N L⁻² T⁻¹] = J ✓.

Across a membrane. If the membrane has thickness Δx and area A, the gradient becomes ΔC/Δx and, multiplying by the area, you get the total flux: J · A = D · A · ΔC / Δx, in mol·s⁻¹ [N T⁻¹]. This is the form to remember: more area and more concentration difference raise the flux, more thickness lowers it.

B · The form for gases: pulmonary exchange

V̇gas = D · A · (P1 − P2) / T

For a gas you measure partial pressure instead of concentration, and the result is a volume of gas per minute instead of moles per second. Mind the symbol: here T is the thickness of the barrier, not time nor temperature. The alveolar-capillary barrier is built for Fick: a huge area, 50–100 m², and a minimal thickness, 0.2–0.5 µm over most of it.

V̇gasvolume of gas crossing the barrier each minute (the result)mL·min⁻¹[L³ T⁻¹]
Ddiffusion coefficient of the gas: proportional to solubility / √molecular weightcm²·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹[M⁻¹ L³ T]
Aalveolar surface aream²[L²]
P1 − P2partial pressure gradient between alveolus and bloodmmHg[M L⁻¹ T⁻²]
Tthickness of the alveolar-capillary barrierµm[L]

[M] mass. Check: [M⁻¹ L³ T] × [L²] × [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] / [L] = [L³ T⁻¹] = V̇ ✓. 1 mmHg ≈ 133.3 Pa (Pa = N/m²).

Diffusing capacity. D, A and T together are a property of the lung: DL = D · A / T, in mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹ [M⁻¹ L⁴ T], so that V̇gas = DL · (P1 − P2). It has the same dimensions as Starling's Kf: both say how much passes per mmHg of driving force.

3 · Darcy’s law: flow through a porous layer

What it is and why it is useful

What it is. Darcy’s law says how much liquid crosses a porous layer. A liquid crosses a layer full of pores (sand, a membrane, a capillary wall) faster the more permeable (k) and the wider (A) the layer is and the stronger the push (ΔP); slower the more viscous the liquid (μ) and the thicker the layer (L). Darcy derived it in 1856 from the sand filters of the fountains of Dijon. It holds for slow, orderly flow.

Why it is useful. To calculate how much fluid filters across a wall for a given push. It is the law behind glomerular filtration (GFR = Kf · ΔP), and it explains where the Kf of the Starling equation comes from.

Q = k · A · ΔP / (μ · L)

The variables

SymbolWhat it isEffect on flowSI unitDimensions
Qflow: volume crossing the layer per unit time (the result)m³·s⁻¹ (in physiology mL·min⁻¹)[L³ T⁻¹]
kpermeability of the layer: how many pores, how widemore pores, more flowm²[L²]
Aarea crossedmore area, more flowm²[L²]
ΔPpressure difference between the two sidesit is the pushPa (Pa = N/m²; N = kg·m/s²)[M L⁻¹ T⁻²]
μviscosity of the liquidmore viscous, less flowPa·s[M L⁻¹ T⁻¹]
Lthickness of the layerthicker, less flowm[L]

[M] mass, [L] length, [T] time. Check: [L²] × [L²] × [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] / ([M L⁻¹ T⁻¹] × [L]) = [L³ T⁻¹] = Q ✓. 1 mmHg ≈ 133.3 Pa.

From Darcy to glomerular filtration, in four steps

  1. Darcy.Q = k · A · ΔP / (μ · L)
  2. Group everything that describes the wall and the liquid. Per unit area:Lp = k / (μ · L)Lp = hydraulic conductivity [M⁻¹ L² T], m·s⁻¹·Pa⁻¹. Multiplied by the area:Kf = Lp · A = k · A / (μ · L)Kf = filtration coefficient [M⁻¹ L⁴ T], m³·s⁻¹·Pa⁻¹ (in physiology mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹).
  3. Darcy becomes a single line.Q = Kf · ΔPIt is the same shape as Starling: there the push ΔP is the net one, hydrostatic minus oncotic pressure.
  4. In the glomerulus, with numbers. Kf ≈ 12.5 mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹, ΔP net ≈ 10 mmHg:Q = 12.5 × 10 = 125 mL·min⁻¹This is the glomerular filtration rate.

Who moves which term, in the glomerulus

TermWhat changes itEffect
Athe mesangium contracting closes part of the capillary loops; loss of nephronsless area: Kf and filtration fall
Lthickening of the basement membrane (for example in diabetes)more thickness: Kf falls
kpores of the endothelium, mesh of the basement membrane, slits between the foot processesfewer pores: Kf falls
ΔPafferent and efferent arterioles; plasma oncotic pressure; pressure in the capsulechanges the push, not Kf

Layers in series. The glomerular barrier has three layers one after the other: endothelium, basement membrane, slits between the foot processes. Each layer offers a resistance μ · L / (k · A), and resistances in series add up: flow is ΔP divided by the total resistance, like current in Ohm’s law.

Same shape as Fick. Flow = coefficient × area × push / thickness. In Fick a solute moves, pushed by a difference in concentration or partial pressure; in Darcy the whole liquid moves, pushed by a pressure difference.

To try it: on the portal circulations page the kidney figure starts from Darcy and lets you move pressures and thicknesses. The mesangium and the vascular pole are drawn in the nephron figure of the transporters page.

Indicative values, for teaching. The net pressure of 10 mmHg is the mean along the glomerular capillary.
