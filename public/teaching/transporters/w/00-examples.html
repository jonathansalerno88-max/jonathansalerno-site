Six examples, one method

  1. Choose the substance and the direction: where it starts, where it arrives. In the drawings the chequered flag marks the arrival.
  2. Work out its terms: concentration, electrical, pressure. Always arrival minus departure.
  3. Add them up: that is the ΔG transport. Negative, it goes by itself.
  4. If it is positive, add who pays: the total must be negative.
Legend and values used
  • ΔG = free energy per mole [energy ÷ amount of substance], J/mol (J = N·m); here in kJ/mol
  • R·T = gas constant × absolute temperature = 8.314 J/(mol·K) × 310 K = 2 577 J/mol
  • c arrival ÷ c departure = ratio of the concentrations [pure number]
  • z = charge of the ion [pure number]
  • F = Faraday constant, 96 485 C/mol (C = A·s)
  • Δφ = voltage at arrival − voltage at departure, V (V = J/C)
  • ΔP = hydrostatic pressure at arrival − at departure; here in mmHg (1 mmHg = 133 Pa, Pa = N/m²)
  • ΔΠ = osmotic pressure at arrival − at departure, mmHg
  • V̄ = volume of one mole of substance, m³/mol

1Glucose enters muscle (GLUT)

Does it enter by itself? With how much push?

1Substance and direction. Glucose, from outside to inside: the arrival is the inside of the cell.

2Which terms count. Concentration: yes. Electrical: no, glucose has no charge (z = 0). Pressure: negligible.

3The sum.

ΔG = R·T·ln(c arrival ÷ c departure) = 2 577 × ln(1 ÷ 5) = −4.15 kJ/mol

R·T = gas constant × temperature, 2 577 J/mol at 310 K · c = concentrations, mmol/L

4Verdict. ΔG negative: it enters by itself. PASSIVE transport. It would stop at equal concentrations, but the cell phosphorylates glucose as soon as it enters, so inside there is always little.

2K⁺ at rest: does it leave or stay?

Concentration pushes it out, the negative inside holds it back. Who wins?

1Substance and direction. K⁺, from inside to outside: the arrival is the outside.

2Concentration term. There is less outside: it helps it leave.

R·T·ln(c arrival ÷ c departure) = 2 577 × ln(4 ÷ 140) = −9.16 kJ/mol

R·T = gas constant × temperature, 2 577 J/mol at 310 K · c = concentrations, mmol/L

3Electrical term. Δφ = arrival − departure = 0 − (−0.080) = +0.080 V. A positive ion going towards the more positive side: it costs.

z·F·Δφ = +1 × 96 485 × (+0.080 V) = +7.72 kJ/mol

z = charge of the ion · F = Faraday constant, C/mol (C = A·s) · Δφ = voltage at arrival − at departure, V (V = J/C)

4Sum.

ΔG = −9.16 +7.72 = −1.44 kJ/mol

ΔG = concentration term + electrical term, kJ/mol

5Verdict. Only just negative: K⁺ leaves, but slowly. PASSIVE transport. It would stop leaving with the inside at −95 mV: there the two terms cancel. It is the equilibrium potential of K⁺ (Nernst).

3Na⁺ at rest: how much does it want to enter?

Here concentration and voltage push the same way.

1Substance and direction. Na⁺, from outside to inside: the arrival is the inside.

2Concentration term. There is less inside: it helps.

R·T·ln(c arrival ÷ c departure) = 2 577 × ln(12 ÷ 145) = −6.42 kJ/mol

R·T = gas constant × temperature, 2 577 J/mol at 310 K · c = concentrations, mmol/L

3Electrical term. Δφ = arrival − departure = −0.080 − 0 = −0.080 V. A positive ion towards the negative side: this helps too.

z·F·Δφ = +1 × 96 485 × (−0.080 V) = −7.72 kJ/mol

z = charge of the ion · F = Faraday constant, C/mol (C = A·s) · Δφ = voltage at arrival − at departure, V (V = J/C)

4Sum.

ΔG = −6.42 −7.72 = −14.14 kJ/mol

ΔG = concentration term + electrical term, kJ/mol

5Verdict. Very negative: as soon as it finds an open channel, it enters. PASSIVE transport. This push of about 14 kJ/mol is the reserve the cell uses to pay for other transports (example 5).

4The Na⁺/K⁺ pump

Both ions go uphill. Is one ATP enough to pay?

1Substances and direction. It is the reverse of examples 2 and 3: Na⁺ leaves, K⁺ enters. Same numbers, sign flipped.

2Na⁺ leaving. n = number of ions per cycle = 3.

3 × (+14.14) = +42.42 kJ

n × ΔG of one ion; n = number of ions per cycle

3K⁺ entering. n = 2.

2 × (+1.44) = +2.89 kJ

n × ΔG of one ion; n = number of ions per cycle

4ΔG transport. Positive: it does not go by itself.

ΔG transport = 42.42 +2.89 = +45.31 kJ

ΔG transport = sum of the ΔG of all the ions moved in one cycle, kJ

5Who pays. The hydrolysis of one ATP in the cell is worth about −55 kJ/mol.

ΔG total = 45.31 −55 = −9.69 kJ

ΔG total = ΔG transport + ΔG of who pays, kJ

6Verdict. Total negative: the pump turns. PRIMARY ACTIVE transport: ATP pays, directly.

5Glucose in the gut (SGLT1)

Glucose must enter where it is already 10 times more concentrated. Who pays?

1Substances and direction. Glucose and 2 Na⁺, together, from the lumen to the cell.

2Glucose. It goes uphill: ΔG positive.

R·T·ln(c arrival ÷ c departure) = 2 577 × ln(10 ÷ 1) = +5.93 kJ/mol

R·T = gas constant × temperature, 2 577 J/mol at 310 K · c = concentrations, mmol/L

3Who pays: the Na⁺ coming in. It is example 3, with n = 2 ions per cycle.

2 × (−14.14) = −28.28 kJ

n × ΔG of one ion; n = number of ions per cycle

4Total.

ΔG total = +5.93 −28.28 = −22.35 kJ

ΔG total = ΔG transport + ΔG of who pays, kJ

5Verdict. Total negative: glucose enters. SECONDARY ACTIVE transport: no ATP here, the Na⁺ gradient pays, which the pump of example 4 had built earlier.

6How far it can go. With 2 Na⁺ glucose can be concentrated up to about 58 000 times. With only 1 Na⁺ (SGLT2, in the kidney) only about 240 times: this is why the number of ions matters.

6Water in the capillary (Starling)

Does water leave the capillary? It depends on where you look.

1Substance and direction. Water, from capillary to interstitium: the arrival is the interstitium.

2Which terms count. No charge, so no electrical term. Hydrostatic and osmotic pressure remain: ΔG = V̄·(ΔP − ΔΠ). V̄ is the same for both, so the bracket decides the sign, and we can stay in mmHg.

3ΔΠ. arrival − departure = 3 − 28 = −25 mmHg. Of the 28 in plasma, about two thirds are the proteins and one third the ions held back through the Donnan effect.

4Arterial end (P in the capillary 35): ΔP = 0 − 35.

ΔP − ΔΠ = (−35) − (−25) = −10 mmHg

ΔP = hydrostatic pressure, arrival − departure · ΔΠ = osmotic pressure, arrival − departure; mmHg

5Venous end (P in the capillary 15): ΔP = 0 − 15.

ΔP − ΔΠ = (−15) − (−25) = +10 mmHg

ΔP = hydrostatic pressure, arrival − departure · ΔΠ = osmotic pressure, arrival − departure; mmHg

6Verdict. At the arterial end negative: water leaves (filtration). At the venous end positive in this direction: water goes the other way, it comes back in. Always PASSIVE: only which of the two terms prevails changes. This is the classical Starling scheme; in real capillaries less comes back and lymph returns the rest.

Concentrations, voltages and pressures are typical rounded values; the ΔG of ATP (about −55 kJ/mol) is a typical value for a muscle cell.
