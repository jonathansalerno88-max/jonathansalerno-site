A substance runs downhill in free energy by itself; to make it go up something else must go down further: passive, primary active, secondary active.

  1. An environment has to exchange matter. But which way does a substance go?
  2. Free energy, G, decides. Think of G as a height: everything runs down by itself towards the lower level. The drop is ΔG: going down, it is negative.
  3. The transporter does not change the drop: it only makes the passage fast enough. Which way is set by ΔG; how fast is set by the transporter.
  4. Passive: the substance goes down, ΔG < 0. Nobody pays.
  5. Active: the substance goes up, ΔG > 0. It would not go by itself: the transporter ties it to another process that goes down further, so the total stays negative.

Where G comes from: three steps

1 · First law: energy is conserved

Every physical system has an internal energy. It can exchange it with its surroundings in two ways: as heat, if there is a temperature difference, or as work, that is, a push times a displacement. The push can be a force, a pressure, a voltage or a concentration difference. The first law says the books always balance:

ΔU = q − w

  • ΔU = change in the internal energy of the system [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²)
  • q = heat entering the system [M L² T⁻²], J
  • w = work done by the system [M L² T⁻²], J

It holds for a system that does not exchange matter. Some textbooks write ΔU = q + w: there w is the work done on the system.

2 · Second law: the direction

The first law does not say which way things go: glucose coming in and glucose going out both obey it. The direction comes from the second law: what happens by itself is what raises the overall disorder, that is, the entropy of the system plus that of the surroundings.

ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings > 0

  • S = entropy: a measure of how many ways the system can arrange itself [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K

3 · G: the second law looking at the system only

In a cell, temperature and pressure are constant. Then the heat the system gives to the surroundings raises their entropy, and everything can be rewritten with quantities of the system alone:

1ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings > 0the second law

2ΔSsurroundings = −ΔH / Tat constant T and P the surroundings gain the entropy of the heat they receive

3ΔS − ΔH / T > 0substitute

4ΔH − T ΔS < 0multiply by −T: the inequality flips

5ΔG = ΔH − T ΔS < 0call this quantity ΔG

  • H = enthalpy: the heat exchanged at constant pressure [M L² T⁻²], J
  • T = absolute temperature [Θ], K
  • S = entropy: a measure of how many ways the system can arrange itself [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K
  • G = Gibbs free energy [M L² T⁻²], J

G holds two tendencies together: towards less energy (ΔH) and towards more disorder (TΔS). In the transport of an uncharged substance only the second is at work: the molecules do not 'release energy', they spread out because the spread-out state is far more probable. How to compute the ΔG of a substance crossing a membrane is the subject of the next stage.
