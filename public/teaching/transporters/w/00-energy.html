A substance runs downhill in free energy by itself; to make it go up something else must go down further: passive, primary active, secondary active.

  1. Every cell exchanges matter with what surrounds it. In doing so it cannot break three balances: of mass, of charge and of energy. Nothing is created and nothing is destroyed: what comes in equals what goes out, plus what builds up.

The three fundamental balances: what comes in equals what goes out

1ṁin = ṁoutmass: this is Lavoisier's principle (1789)

1aṅi,in + Ri = ṅi,outmass, looked at one substance at a time. Ri = the moles that have not entered, but are produced inside that compartment; if they are used up instead, it is negative

2Σi zi ṅi,in = Σi zi ṅi,outcharge: as many charges come in as go out

3Ėin = Ėoutenergy

3aUA , UBstart here: each compartment has its own internal energy U, that of molecular agitation and of chemical bonds [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²)

3bHA , HB   H = U + P·Vbut we are transferring matter, so we speak of enthalpy H: to internal energy it adds P·V, the energy it took to make room for the matter at that pressure. This way volume work (PV) is accounted for once and for all: it is negligible in a cell, which does not change volume; fundamental in a piston and in the heart, whose work is exactly the area of the pressure-volume loop

3cHbefore = Hafterthen we transfer matter from A to B. If the system exchanged nothing with the outside, neither heat nor work, the total enthalpy would stay the same: it would only move from A to B. And if it exchanged only work and not a bit of heat? Congratulations, you have built the perfect piston: it is called an adiabatic transformation, and it is every engineer's dream. The cell cannot afford it

3dHbefore = q + wnon PV + Hafterin reality something is exchanged. The enthalpy missing at the end has left as heat (q) or as non-PV work (electrical, transport, mechanical): −ΔH = q + wnon PV. For the whole human this is the balance Atwater measured in 1899: The greats of medicine →

3eΔH = Hafter − Hbefore = 400 J − 500 J = −100 Ja worked example, with chosen numbers: at the end the system has 100 J less. They have left, as heat and work together: q + wnon PV = 100 J. It is the same ΔH we shall use in the sums below

So in a cell Hafter cannot be the same as Hbefore: we have a ΔH! Now: of this ΔH (in the example, 100 J), how much can go into non-PV work and how much MUST go into heat? This is THE question, because it is exactly the one entropy was born from. The balance does not say: any split suits it, even all of it as heat.

The same exchange, with one more piece of data: the temperature T at which it happens [Θ], K. In a cell it is 310 K, that is 37 °C.

Now the ratio: I take the heat exchanged and divide it by the temperature at which it was exchanged.

It is what fixes the share that MUST go into heat: just multiply it back by the temperature. T × ΔS = 310 K × 0.1 J/K = 31 J.

Keep them in mind and look below: the magic.

But you want to know the direction of TRANSPORT, right? This entropy is only one piece of the puzzle: it tells you how much heat MUST be exchanged, but not yet which way the process goes. So multiply ΔS by its temperature and apply this formula:

ΔG=ΔH − T·ΔS

Congratulations: you have the ΔG.

ΔG < 0the TRANSPORT goes by itself in that direction
ΔG > 0it does not go by itself: someone must pay

With the numbers of the example: ΔH = −100 J is the one worked out above. The 31 J of heat have left the system, so its entropy has gone down: ΔS = −0.1 J/K.

ΔG = −100 J − (310 K × −0.1 J/K) = −69 J

ΔG is negative: the TRANSPORT goes by itself. And of the 100 J, 31 J MUST leave as heat and at most 69 J can become non-PV work: this is the answer to the earlier question.

ΔH, T·ΔS, ΔG = energies [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²) · ΔS = entropy [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K

So now we have the concepts of enthalpy and entropy, and we know how these physical quantities are tied to ΔG. That is a big piece of information: if something goes wrong in a tissue, we know which way the TRANSPORT will go, and we can predict whether the inside of the cell becomes more positive or more negative, with the biological consequences that follow, especially in excitable tissues.

We know, for example, that the heart, to contract, and neurons, to carry a signal, must reach a certain potential of the cell. And we know that calcium, sodium and potassium channels are mostly what is involved. If I take a drug, or want to develop a new one, that inhibits these channels or on the contrary keeps them open, I would like to know whether the net effect on the tissue is to activate it or to inhibit it. But the direction of the TRANSPORT is not enough: I want to know direction and intensity. Here ΔG alone is no longer enough: we need another quantity.
