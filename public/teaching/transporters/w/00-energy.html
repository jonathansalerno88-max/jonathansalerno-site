A substance runs downhill in free energy by itself; to make it go up something else must go down further: passive, primary active, secondary active.

FIRST STEPSET THE BALANCES

  1. Every cell exchanges matter with what surrounds it. In doing so it cannot break three balances: of mass, of charge and of energy. Nothing is created and nothing is destroyed: what comes in equals what goes out, plus what builds up.

The three fundamental balances: what comes in equals what goes out

1ṁin = ṁoutmass: this is Lavoisier's principle (1789)

1aṅi,in + Ri = ṅi,outmass, looked at one substance at a time. Ri = the moles that have not entered, but are produced inside that compartment; if they are used up instead, it is negative

2Σi zi ṅi,in = Σi zi ṅi,outcharge: as many charges come in as go out

3Ėin = Ėoutenergy

3aUA , UBstart here: each compartment has its own internal energy U, that of molecular agitation and of chemical bonds [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²)

3bHA , HB   H = U + P·Vbut we are transferring matter, so we speak of enthalpy H: to internal energy it adds P·V, the energy it took to make room for the matter at that pressure. This way volume work (PV) is accounted for once and for all: it is negligible in a cell, which does not change volume; fundamental in a piston and in the heart, whose work is exactly the area of the pressure-volume loop

3cHbefore = Hafterthen we transfer matter from A to B. If the system exchanged nothing with the outside, neither heat nor work, the total enthalpy would stay the same: it would only move from A to B. And if it exchanged only work and not a bit of heat? Congratulations, you have built the perfect piston: it is called an adiabatic transformation, and it is every engineer's dream. The cell cannot afford it

3dHbefore = q + wnon PV + Hafterin reality something is exchanged. The enthalpy missing at the end has left as heat (q) or as non-PV work (electrical, transport, mechanical): −ΔH = q + wnon PV. For the whole human this is the balance Atwater measured in 1899: The greats of medicine →

3eΔH = Hafter − Hbefore = 400 J − 500 J = −100 Ja worked example, with chosen numbers: at the end the system has 100 J less. They have left, as heat and work together: q + wnon PV = 100 J. It is the same ΔH we shall use in the sums below

So in a cell Hafter cannot be the same as Hbefore: we have a ΔH! ΔH = −100 J

Of these 100 J, how much can go into non-PV work and how much MUST go into heat?

This is THE question, because it is exactly the one entropy was born from. The balance does not say: any split suits it, even all of it as heat.

SECOND STEPFIND THE ENTROPY CHANGE

The same exchange, done at three different temperatures. Each time I measure the heat exchanged and divide it by the temperature at which it was exchanged.

28 J ÷ 280 K = 0.1 J/K

31 J ÷ 310 K = 0.1 J/K

34 J ÷ 340 K = 0.1 J/K

The heat changes, the ratio does not. Congratulations: you have found the entropy change, ΔS.

[M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K (J = kg·m²/s²)

It is what fixes the share that MUST go into heat: just multiply it back by the temperature. T × ΔS = 310 K × 0.1 J/K = 31 J.

THIRD STEPFIND THE ΔG

So now we have three numbers:

ΔH = −100 Jthe enthalpy changeΔS = −0.1 J/Kthe entropy change
with a minus, because the heat left the systemT = 310 Kthe temperature at which the exchange happens
37 °C, body temperature

Keep them in mind and look below: the magic.

But you want to know the direction of TRANSPORT, right? This entropy is only one piece of the puzzle: it tells you how much heat MUST be exchanged, but not yet which way the process goes. So multiply ΔS by its temperature and apply this formula:

ΔGCAN go into non-PV work
(transport, or whatever you want)=ΔH − T·ΔS

Congratulations: you have the ΔG.

ΔG < 0the TRANSPORT goes by itself in that direction
ΔG > 0it does not go by itself: someone must pay (e.g. ATP)

Example: Ca²⁺ leaves the sarcoplasmic reticulum for the cytosol. In the reticulum it is about 10 000 times more concentrated: ΔG ≪ 0. Open the channel and it runs down by itself, all at once.

CONTRACTION

What sets it off is the depolarisation: it pays for nothing, it only opens the channel.

What ΔG depends on

  • R = gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K)
  • T = absolute temperature, K
  • c₂ ÷ c₁ = concentration at arrival ÷ concentration at departure
  • z = charge of the ion
  • F = Faraday constant, 96 485 C/mol (C = A·s)
  • Δφ = voltage at arrival − voltage at departure, V (V = J/C)
  • V̄ = volume of one mole of substance, m³/mol
  • ΔP = difference in hydrostatic pressure, Pa (Pa = N/m²): for example the one due to the cell wall, if there is one, or the hydrostatic term of Starling in filtration from capillary to interstitium
  • ΔΠ = difference in osmotic pressure (Π = R·T·c, in Pa): it is the concentration term of water, written as a pressure
  • Donnan: Δφ = −(R·T ÷ z·F)·ln(c₂ ÷ c₁), the same for all the ions that can cross. It holds at equilibrium, when on one side there are charges that cannot cross (proteins): the ΔG transport of every ion that crosses is zero
  • Goldman: Δφ = (R·T ÷ F)·ln[(PK·[K⁺]out + PNa·[Na⁺]out + PCl·[Cl⁻]in) ÷ (PK·[K⁺]in + PNa·[Na⁺]in + PCl·[Cl⁻]out)], with Δφ = inside − outside; P = permeability of the membrane to that ion [L ÷ T], m/s. It holds in the steady state (living cell): the ΔG transport of each ion stays different from zero, and the Na⁺/K⁺ pump pays
  • set from outside: a battery or an electrode, as in the drawing on the right
  • ATP: ΔG°′ = −30.5 kJ/mol (standard conditions); Q = [ADP]·[P] ÷ [ATP]; in the cell the total is about −55 kJ/mol
  • redox: n = number of electrons exchanged in the reaction (not the moles: e.g. 2); ΔE = difference in redox potential between who takes them and who gives them, V (V = J/C)
  • other ions: the same formula as the transport, applied to the ion that pays (usually Na⁺); n = number of ions that cross at each cycle of the transporter (not the moles: e.g. 2 Na⁺ per glucose, so n = 2)

ΔG transport > 0 it does not go by itself

ΔG transport > 0 + ΔG ATP ≪ 0 = ΔG total < 0

RELAXATION

It does not happen in vivo: in the cell only ATP pays here. In the laboratory, on isolated membrane, it is really done. The principle is the same: a voltage can supply the missing ΔG.

ΔG transport > 0 it does not go by itself

ΔG transport < 0 it runs down by itself: nobody pays, you changed the gradient

It does not happen in vivo: Ca²⁺ in the cytosol always stays far below that of the reticulum. It serves to see the principle: if you change the concentrations, you change the sign of ΔG.

Now we have the general principles that govern transport, and we can apply them to the various situations. The point is just one: the overall ΔG must be negative. And every process involved has to go into the count: for example ATP hydrolysis, the potential of a battery, or the new electrochemical potential given by the ions I poured in.
