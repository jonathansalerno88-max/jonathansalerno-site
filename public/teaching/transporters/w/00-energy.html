A substance runs downhill in free energy by itself; to make it go up something else must go down further: passive, primary active, secondary active.

  1. Every cell exchanges matter with what surrounds it. In doing so it cannot break three balances: of mass, of charge and of energy. Nothing is created and nothing is destroyed: what comes in equals what goes out, plus what builds up.

The three fundamental balances: what comes in equals what goes out

1ṁin = ṁoutmass: this is Lavoisier's principle (1789)

1aṅi,in + Ri = ṅi,outmass, looked at one substance at a time. Ri = the moles that have not entered, but are produced inside that compartment; if they are used up instead, it is negative

2Σi zi ṅi,in = Σi zi ṅi,outcharge: as many charges come in as go out

3aUA , UBstart here: each compartment has its own internal energy U, that of molecular agitation and of chemical bonds [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²)

3bHA , HB   H = U + P·Vbut we are transferring matter, so we speak of enthalpy H: to internal energy it adds P·V, the energy it took to make room for the matter at that pressure. This way volume work (PV) is accounted for once and for all: it is negligible in a cell, which does not change volume; fundamental in a piston and in the heart, whose work is exactly the area of the pressure-volume loop

3cHbefore = Hafterthen we transfer matter from A to B. If the system exchanged nothing with the outside, neither heat nor work, the total enthalpy would stay the same: it would only move from A to B. And if it exchanged only work and not a bit of heat? Congratulations, you have built the perfect piston: it is called an adiabatic transformation, and it is every engineer's dream. The cell cannot afford it

3dHbefore = q + wnon PV + Hafterin reality something is exchanged. The enthalpy missing at the end has left as heat (q) or as non-PV work (electrical, transport, mechanical): −ΔH = q + wnon PV. For the whole human this is the balance Atwater measured in 1899: The greats of medicine →

So in a cell Hafter cannot be the same as Hbefore: we have a ΔH! Now: of this ΔH, how much can go into non-PV work and how much MUST go into heat? The balance does not say: any split suits it, even all of it as heat.

The same exchange, with one more piece of data: the temperature T at which it happens [Θ], K. In a cell it is 310 K, that is 37 °C.

Whether you want a machine that produces as much heat as possible, or one that produces as little as possible so as to get work out of it, you give that 0.1 J/K back no matter what. You can give back more, never less: this is the second law. Example: 62 J come in at 620 K (0.1 J/K). The ideal engine dumps 31 J at 310 K (0.1 J/K) and the other 31 J are work. The stove dumps all 62 (0.2 J/K): no work.

But you want to know the direction, right? This entropy is only one piece of the puzzle: it tells you how much heat MUST be exchanged, but not yet which way the process goes. So multiply ΔS by its temperature and apply this formula:

ΔH − T·ΔS

Congratulations: you have the ΔG. Let us try it in two cases.

1 · Transport with new bonds being formed

As it crosses, the substance changes its bonds: in bacteria glucose is phosphorylated while it goes through (group translocation), or on the other side it binds to a protein. Round example numbers: 100 J are given off, and the heat that left lowers the entropy of the system.

ΔH = −100 J · ΔS = −0.1 J/K

About 5 mmol of glucose run through a GLUT from where they are 10 times more concentrated. No bond changes, so ΔH = 0.

OOPS! No heat to exchange: so how do I calculate ΔS?

Entropy depends only on where you start and where you end up. The substance spreads out, and for dilution there is a ready-made formula: it gives the same result as q ÷ T, had the dilution been done in the ideal way.

ΔS = n R ln(cA / cB) = 0.0052 mol × 8.314 J/(mol·K) × ln 10 = +0.1 J/K

Bonus: Boltzmann's method, by counting
The idea

Heat is energy that comes in in a disordered way. When you heat something, the energy does not go to all the molecules in the same way: it is spread among them at random, as agitation. And the more energy there is to spread, the more ways there are of sharing it out among the molecules. So giving heat raises the number of ways.

Same case 2, a third route: no heat, you count the ways. When the concentration drops 10-fold, each molecule has 10 times more places to be in. With N molecules the ways of arranging them are multiplied by 10 × 10 × 10… N times.

Why exactly 10? Concentration is the number of molecules in a given volume. If it is 10 times lower, the same volume is shared by 10 times fewer molecules: each has 10 times more room, that is, 10 times more free places. So the gain in places is the ratio of the concentrations:

WB ÷ WA = cA ÷ cB = 10

Note the inversion: for the places B is on top, for the concentrations A is on top. Places and concentration go in opposite directions: where the concentration is lower, there are more places.

1S = k ln Wentropy measures the number of ways W in which the molecules can arrange themselves

2ΔS = k ln(WB / WA) = k ln(10N) = N k ln 10all that counts is by how many times the ways have grown: 10 for each molecule

3ΔS = 3.1 × 10²¹ × 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K × ln 10 = +0.1 J/Kwith the numbers of case 2

ΔH, T·ΔS, ΔG = energies [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²) · ΔS = entropy [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K

In both cases ΔG is negative: the process goes by itself, from A to B. In the first it is mostly the energy of the bonds (ΔH) that pushes, in the second only entropy (T·ΔS). ΔG puts them together in a single number, and that is what gives the direction.

Well done: now we know the spontaneous direction. But… when does the process stop?

ΔG = 0

Want to guess what has ΔG = 0? Dead things.

A living cell, taken as a whole, never gets to ΔG = 0. When everything looks still it is not at equilibrium: it has reached a stationary state.

Picture it as a ping-pong rally. The ball goes back and forth only as long as the two players keep hitting it. In the cell, ions run down on one side through channels, and the pump sends them back by spending ATP. If the players stop, the ball drops to the floor and stays there: that is ΔG = 0.

Careful: this holds for the cell as a whole, not for every substance. Single substances can be at equilibrium: water, which crosses the membrane so fast that it always evens out; chloride in many cells, which nobody pumps. But it is a borrowed equilibrium: they are still with respect to a voltage that exists only because the pump is working. If the pump stops, they start moving again. Each single substance can be still; the whole cannot, as long as the cell is alive.

There was a little lie. If there were a single substance, the process would go on until ΔG = 0. But in reality there is a mixture of many substances and ions, and each one 'wants' to take the cell to its own end point… like a tug of war.

So, to predict where the stationary state is reached, we need a ΔG for each substance, for one mole. It has a name: chemical potential, μ.

The formula for μ, one piece at a time

Why do we need μ, if we already have ΔG? ΔG gives you the condition, not the place. It tells you 'equilibrium is where ΔG = 0'. But that is like saying 'you have arrived when you have arrived': it does not tell you at which concentrations and at which voltage it happens. This is what μ is for: it writes ΔG, for one mole of one substance, in terms of what you can measure, namely concentrations and voltages. That way 'equal to zero' becomes an equation you can solve.

First piece: concentration. For a substance with no charge, like glucose, you have already worked it out: it is the ΔG of case 2, divided by the moles.

1Δμ = μB − μA = R T ln(cB / cA)how much μ changes going from A to B: negative if B is less concentrated, and then the substance goes there by itself

1aΔμ = 8.314 J/(mol·K) × 310 K × ln(1/10) = −5.9 kJ/molwith the 10-fold gradient of case 2
