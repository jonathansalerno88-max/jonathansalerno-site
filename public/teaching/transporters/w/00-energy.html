A substance runs downhill in free energy by itself; to make it go up something else must go down further: passive, primary active, secondary active.

  1. Every cell exchanges matter with what surrounds it. In doing so it cannot break three balances: of mass, of charge and of energy. Nothing is created and nothing is destroyed: what comes in equals what goes out, plus what builds up.
  2. But the balances do not say which way a substance goes. Free energy, G, decides: for a substance, it is its chemical potential μ. Think of G as a height: everything runs down by itself towards the lower level. The drop is ΔG: going down, it is negative.
  3. The transporter does not change the drop: it only makes the passage fast enough. Which way is set by ΔG; how fast is set by the transporter.
  4. Passive: the substance goes down, ΔG < 0. Nobody pays.
  5. Active: the substance goes up, ΔG > 0. It would not go by itself: the transporter ties it to another process that goes down further, so the total stays negative.

The three fundamental balances: what comes in equals what goes out

1ṁin = ṁoutmass

1aṅi,in + Ri = ṅi,outmass, looked at one substance at a time: here reactions count too, since they make it or use it up

2Σi zi ṅi,in = Σi zi ṅi,outcharge: as many charges come in as go out

3Ėin = Ėoutenergy

3aĖnutrients = q̇ + ẇ + Ėwasteenergy, spelled out: it comes in with nutrients and leaves as heat, as work or inside waste products

There are three fundamental balances: 1, 2 and 3. The indented lines are not extra balances: 1a is mass seen substance by substance, 3a is energy spelled out.

  • ṁin, ṁout = mass coming in or going out per unit time [M T⁻¹], kg/s
  • ṅi,in, ṅi,out = moles of substance i coming in or going out per unit time [N T⁻¹], mol/s
  • Ri = moles of substance i made (or used up, if negative) by reactions per unit time [N T⁻¹], mol/s · zi = charge of ion i, a pure number
  • Ėin, Ėout = energy coming in or going out per unit time, in any form: inside matter (nutrients, waste products), as heat, as work [M L² T⁻³], W (W = J/s)
  • Ėnutrients = chemical energy of the nutrients coming in · Ėwaste = chemical energy of the waste products going out · q̇ = heat given to the surroundings · ẇ = work done (mechanical, electrical, transport); all per unit time [M L² T⁻³], W

They hold exactly like this for a cell that is neither growing nor emptying (steady state). If the cell builds something up, what builds up is added on the right: for mass, substances and energy it can be a lot (a growing cell, a fat store); for charge it is always tiny, just the charge layer on the membrane. Transporters live in the in and out terms: they decide how large these are.

Energy in an open system: what matter carries with it

In the energy balance of an open system, the energy carried by the matter crossing the boundary must be counted too. That energy, for each mole, is the enthalpy.

The cell is an open system: energy does not come in and go out only as heat and work, but also inside the matter that crosses the boundary. Each mole that passes carries its own internal energy, plus the work needed to push it across the boundary against the pressure there (flow work). The sum of the two is the enthalpy.

1h = u + P vper mole: internal energy plus flow work

2Σi ṅi,in hi = q̇ + ẇ + Σi ṅi,out hibalance 3a, written with what matter really carries: its enthalpy

  • h = enthalpy of one mole of substance [M L² T⁻² N⁻¹], J/mol (J = kg·m²/s²)
  • u = internal energy of one mole of substance [M L² T⁻² N⁻¹], J/mol
  • P = pressure at the boundary [M L⁻¹ T⁻²], Pa (Pa = N/m²) · v = volume taken up by one mole [L³ N⁻¹], m³/mol
  • P v = flow work: the work done to push one mole across the boundary [M L² T⁻² N⁻¹], J/mol

Enthalpy is not 'the heat', and it is not the internal energy: it is a third quantity, which coincides with one or the other only in special cases. The two most used ones hold for a closed system:

aQ = ΔHat constant pressure, if the only work is volume work: the heat exchanged is the change in enthalpy

bQ = ΔUat constant volume, if no work is done: the heat exchanged is the change in internal energy

Case b is the bomb calorimeter, used to measure the energy content of a substance by burning it: it measures ΔU, not ΔH. The two differ by the term Δ(PV), which matters when the number of moles of gas changes in the reaction. For the derivation of Q = ΔH at constant pressure: NASA Glenn Research Center, Enthalpy.

Why enthalpy is not enough: entropy and G

Lowering enthalpy is not the same as 'spontaneous process'. In the nineteenth century many held that it was, Julius Thomsen and Marcellin Berthelot above all: a reaction would go by itself whenever it releases heat. Two counter-examples show otherwise.

  • It releases heat, but is not always spontaneous. The synthesis of ammonia, N₂ + 3 H₂ ⇌ 2 NH₃, releases heat (ΔH° = −92 kJ). Yet, under standard conditions, it is spontaneous only below about 190 °C: higher up it no longer is.
  • It absorbs heat, and yet it is spontaneous. Ammonium nitrate dissolving in water cools the glass (ΔH° = +28 kJ/mol), but it dissolves by itself. So do, to a lesser extent, table salt and potassium bromide: a highly ordered crystal lattice disperses into free ions.

A second term was needed. Rudolf Clausius introduced it in 1865: entropy, S. The name comes from the Greek for “transformation”, and at first it had nothing to do with disorder.

How Clausius got there: the same heat is not worth the same at every temperature

Clausius was studying steam engines. Sadi Carnot, in 1824, had understood that work arises when heat 'falls' from hot to cold, like water in a mill. But in a real engine part of the heat falls without doing any work. The energy is all still there, and yet something has been lost for good. What?

The key: 100 joules of heat are not worth the same at every temperature. The hotter they are, the more work can be drawn from them.

1Wmax = Q · (1 − T0 / T)the most work that can be drawn (Carnot)

2S = Q / T  →  100 J / 500 K = 0.20 J/K  ·  100 J / 300 K = 0.33 J/KClausius weighs heat by the temperature at which it is exchanged

3ΔS = 0.33 − 0.20 = +0.13 J/K100 J fallen from 500 K to 300 K without doing work: entropy has gone up

4T0 · ΔS = 300 K × 0.133 J/K = 40 Jexactly the work that can no longer be obtained

  • Q = heat exchanged [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²) · Wmax = the most work that can be drawn from it [M L² T⁻²], J
  • T = temperature at which the heat is available [Θ], K · T0 = lowest temperature at hand, that of the surroundings [Θ], K
  • S = entropy: heat divided by the temperature at which it is exchanged [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K

Energy was conserved: the 100 J are all still there. What was lost is the chance to turn 40 of them into work. Entropy measures this loss, and T·ΔS is the energy that can no longer be used.

It was Ludwig Boltzmann, in 1877, who showed what entropy measures at the level of molecules: in how many ways they can arrange themselves. That is where the idea of entropy as disorder comes from. The second law of thermodynamics says that the total entropy, that of the system plus that of its surroundings, never decreases: it stays constant in a reversible process and increases in an irreversible one. So a process can be spontaneous even if the disorder of the system decreases, provided it releases enough heat to raise that of the surroundings by more. Josiah Willard Gibbs put the two terms into a single quantity, which concerns the system alone:

1ΔG = ΔH − T ΔSGibbs's equation, at constant temperature and pressure

2ΔG = −92.2 kJ − 298 K × (−0.199 kJ/K) = −33 kJammonia at 25 °C: spontaneous

3T = ΔH / ΔS = 92.2 / 0.199 ≈ 464 K ≈ 190 °Cthe temperature at which ΔG = 0: above it, no longer spontaneous

  • ΔG = change in the free energy of the system [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²)
  • ΔH = change in enthalpy: at constant pressure, the heat exchanged [M L² T⁻²], J
  • T = absolute temperature [Θ], K
  • ΔS = change in the entropy of the system [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K

A process is spontaneous if and only if ΔG < 0. At ΔG = 0 there is equilibrium: the process and its reverse go at the same rate.

Of the enthalpy exchanged, the part that cannot be turned into work is TΔS; the part that can is ΔG: that is why it is called 'free' energy. Gibbs introduced it in 1873 as 'available energy' and developed it between 1876 and 1878 in On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances (source 13). The step-by-step derivation is in the box at the bottom.

A complication of G: the chemical potential μ, that is, G per mole of one substance

So far G concerns the whole system. For a transporter the same thing is needed for one substance: how much free energy each of its moles carries. This is the chemical potential μ, which Gibbs introduced in 1876. Counting moles tells how much substance passes; μ tells which way. For an ion, the energy of its charge in the electrical potential where it sits is added.

GG = Σi ni μiG of the whole system: the sum of what each substance carries

1μ = h − T sthe same Gibbs equation, for one mole

2μ̃i = μi + zi F φfor an ion: electrochemical potential

3ΔG = μ̃i,B − μ̃i,Afor each mole going from A to B: the substance goes from where μ̃ is higher to where it is lower

  • G = Gibbs free energy [M L² T⁻²], J · ni = amount of substance i in the cell [N], mol
  • μi = chemical potential: free energy carried by one mole of substance i [M L² T⁻² N⁻¹], J/mol
  • μ̃i = electrochemical potential: μ plus the charge term [M L² T⁻² N⁻¹], J/mol
  • T = absolute temperature [Θ], K · s = entropy of one mole of substance [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹ N⁻¹], J/(mol·K)
  • zi = charge of ion i, a pure number · F = Faraday constant, the charge of a mole of charges [I T N⁻¹], 96 485 C/mol (C = A·s) · φ = electrical potential where the ion sits [M L² T⁻³ I⁻¹], V (V = J/C)
  • ΔG = free energy of one mole going from A to B [M L² T⁻² N⁻¹], J/mol

The charge balance (line 2) also only counts how many charges pass. Which way an ion moves is decided by μ̃: it is the 'electrochemical gradient' of the next stage, where ΔG = RT ln([X]in/[X]out) + zFVm is exactly line 3 spelled out.

The derivation of ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, step by step

1 · First law: energy is conserved

Every physical system has an internal energy. It can exchange it with its surroundings in two ways: as heat, if there is a temperature difference, or as work, that is, a push times a displacement. The push can be a force, a pressure, a voltage or a concentration difference. The first law says the books always balance:

ΔU = q − w

  • ΔU = change in the internal energy of the system [M L² T⁻²], J (J = kg·m²/s²)
  • q = heat entering the system [M L² T⁻²], J
  • w = work done by the system [M L² T⁻²], J

It holds for a system that does not exchange matter. Some textbooks write ΔU = q + w: there w is the work done on the system.

2 · Second law: the direction

The first law does not say which way things go: glucose coming in and glucose going out both obey it. The direction comes from the second law: what happens by itself is what raises the overall disorder, that is, the entropy of the system plus that of the surroundings.

ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings > 0

  • S = entropy: a measure of how many ways the system can arrange itself [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K

3 · G: the second law looking at the system only

In a cell, temperature and pressure are constant. Then the heat the system gives to the surroundings raises their entropy, and everything can be rewritten with quantities of the system alone:

1ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings > 0the second law

2ΔSsurroundings = −ΔH / Tat constant T and P the surroundings gain the entropy of the heat they receive

3ΔS − ΔH / T > 0substitute

4ΔH − T ΔS < 0multiply by −T: the inequality flips

5ΔG = ΔH − T ΔS < 0call this quantity ΔG

  • H = enthalpy: the heat exchanged at constant pressure [M L² T⁻²], J
  • T = absolute temperature [Θ], K
  • S = entropy: a measure of how many ways the system can arrange itself [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹], J/K
  • G = Gibbs free energy [M L² T⁻²], J

G holds two tendencies together: towards less energy (ΔH) and towards more disorder (TΔS). In the transport of an uncharged substance only the second is at work: the molecules do not 'release energy', they spread out because the spread-out state is far more probable. How to compute the ΔG of a substance crossing a membrane is the subject of the next stage.
