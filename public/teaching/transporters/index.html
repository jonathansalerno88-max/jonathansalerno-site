1 · What a transporter is

A transporter is a membrane protein that binds a substance on one side, changes shape and releases it on the other. It is either passive or active.

Passive or active with respect to what? The electrochemical gradient

Two pushes act on a substance crossing the membrane. The chemical push (blue arrow) comes from concentration: the substance tends to move from where it is more concentrated to where it is less. The electrical push (red arrow) comes from the membrane potential and acts only on ions: the inside of the cell is negative, so it attracts cations and repels anions. The sum of the two is the electrochemical gradient (purple arrow).

  • Passive: the substance moves the way the purple arrow points, that is down its electrochemical gradient. No energy is needed.
  • Active: the substance moves against the purple arrow, that is up. It needs energy: ATP (primary active, the pumps) or another ion going down in the same transporter (secondary active).

Concentration alone does not decide. An ion can move against its concentration and still be passive, if the electrical push is stronger. For an uncharged molecule, like glucose, there is no red arrow and the electrochemical gradient is just the chemical one.

Try:
  • K⁺ at −70 mV: the purple arrow points outward and K⁺ leaves through the channels on its own. Switch to “Active transport”: the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase brings it back in, uphill, with ATP.
  • K⁺ at −100 mV: now the purple arrow points inward and K⁺ comes in on its own, even though it is 28 times more concentrated inside.
  • “Set Vm = E”: the red and blue arrows cancel and the net flux is zero (equilibrium, or reversal, potential).
  • Glucose: move the voltage and the red arrow stays at zero.

The formula

Δμ = RT ln(Cin / Cout) + zFVm

first term = blue arrow (chemical) · second term = red arrow (electrical) · sum = purple arrow

  • Δμ = electrochemical potential difference between inside and outside, that is the energy to bring 1 mol of substance from outside to inside [M L² T⁻² N⁻¹] J/mol (J = kg·m²/s²)
  • R = gas constant, 8.314 [M L² T⁻² Θ⁻¹ N⁻¹] J/(mol·K)
  • T = absolute temperature [Θ] K (37 °C = 310 K)
  • Cin = concentration inside the cell [N L⁻³] mol/m³ (1 mM = 1 mol/m³)
  • Cout = concentration outside the cell [N L⁻³] mol/m³
  • z = charge of the ion (valence): K⁺ +1, Cl⁻ −1, Ca²⁺ +2, glucose 0 [1] pure number
  • F = Faraday constant, 96,485: the charge of 1 mol of elementary charges [T I N⁻¹] C/mol (C = A·s)
  • Vm = membrane potential, inside minus outside [M L² T⁻³ I⁻¹] V (V = J/C = kg·m²/(s³·A))
  1. Read the sign. Δμ < 0: coming in releases energy, so the substance enters on its own (passive). Δμ > 0: it leaves on its own, and bringing it in needs energy (active). Δμ = 0: equilibrium.
  2. No charge. With z = 0 the electrical term vanishes: Δμ = RT ln(Cin / Cout).
  3. At equilibrium Δμ = 0, so RT ln(Cin / Cout) = −zFVm, that is Vm = E = (RT / zF) · ln(Cout / Cin) (Nernst equation) E = equilibrium potential of the ion [M L² T⁻³ I⁻¹], V. Units: (J/mol) / (C/mol) = J/C = V ✓; the ratio Cout / Cin is a pure number.
  4. With numbers: K⁺, Cin = 140 mM, Cout = 5 mM, T = 310 K, Vm = −70 mV = −0.070 V RT ln(Cin / Cout) = 8.314 × 310 × ln(140 / 5) = 2577 × 3.33 = +8.59 kJ/mol zFVm = (+1) × 96,485 × (−0.070) = −6.75 kJ/mol Δμ = +8.59 − 6.75 = +1.83 kJ/mol > 0 K⁺ leaves through the channels on its own (passive); bringing it back in takes the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (active). EK = (8.314 × 310) / (1 × 96,485) × ln(5 / 140) = 0.0267 V × (−3.33) = −89 mV At −70 mV the membrane sits above EK, so K⁺ leaves: the same way the purple arrow points in the figure.

2 · How to cross a membrane

The membrane is a lipid bilayer. There are three routes across it: straight through the lipids (simple diffusion, only for small uncharged molecules), through a channel, or carried by a carrier. Then you look inside each route: channels differ in what opens them, carriers in how many substances they carry and in which direction. Simple diffusion and channels are always passive; a carrier can be passive, active (the pump, with ATP) or secondary active.

3 · The transporters of the kidney

Every transporter of the nephron is classified with one question: does the substance go down or up its electrochemical gradient? If it goes down, transport is passive, across the membrane (simple diffusion) or through a channel or a carrier (facilitated diffusion). If it goes up, energy is needed: the pump spends ATP directly (primary active), or the same carrier uses another ion that goes down, usually Na⁺ (secondary active: symport if they go the same way, antiport if opposite). Channels such as ENaC, ROMK and aquaporins are always passive.

The whole kidney, with the arterioles, the RAAS and the sympathetic system, is on the page about portal circulations.

4 · Two bacterial transports: ABC and group translocation

Bacteria have two ways of taking up nutrients that are worth knowing, because they are easily confused with facilitated diffusion. Two questions are enough to tell them apart: who pays the energy, and whether the substance enters unchanged or modified.

  • ABC system (ATP-Binding Cassette): a pump that spends ATP. The substance goes up its gradient but enters unchanged.
  • Group translocation, the PTS (PhosphoTransferase System): the substance is modified as it enters. Glucose enters as glucose-6-phosphate, and the phosphate comes from PEP (phosphoenolpyruvate), not from ATP.

And in humans?

Of the two systems, we have one and not the other.

MechanismIn bacteriaIn humans
Group translocation (PTS)yes: glucose enters already phosphorylated, with energy from PEPno. We use the same trick in two separate steps: glucose enters unchanged through a GLUT (GLUcose Transporter, facilitated diffusion), then inside hexokinase phosphorylates it with ATP. Glucose-6-phosphate cannot leave, and free glucose inside stays low
ABC systemyes, mainly to bring nutrients in, with the binding proteinyes, about 48 genes, but almost all of them pump things out: we lack the binding protein needed for import

5 · Transport or secretion?

In bacteria the two words follow a precise rule, and it is a convention: what crosses decides, not the machine that carries it.

TransportSecretion
What crossessmall molecules: sugars, ions, amino acids, drugsproteins: toxins, enzymes, parts of pili and flagella, effectors to inject
Directionin and outalmost always out
How many membranesthe cytoplasmic membraneone, two in Gram−, sometimes the host cell's too
What forfeeding, keeping ions, defence (efflux)pathogenicity, building external structures, competing with other bacteria

Proof that the machine does not decide: the same ABC transporter (ATP-Binding Cassette) that brings maltose in is a transporter; an ABC protein that exports a protein, haemolysin, is the core of the T1SS (Type 1 Secretion System) and that is secretion. Same mechanism, different cargo, different name.

The grey areas: siderophores, small iron-capturing molecules, are said to be “secreted” but leave through efflux pumps, that is transporters; proteins inserted into the membrane by the Sec system are translocated, not secreted; capsule and LPS (lipopolysaccharide) are said to be exported, by machines of their own. And mind the change of subject: in human physiology “secretion” means that a cell makes and releases something, whatever it is, insulin as well as stomach HCl.

The secretion systems

A Gram+ protein has to cross one membrane; in Gram− bacteria it has to cross the inner membrane, the periplasm, full of enzymes that degrade it, and the outer membrane. This is why Gram− bacteria have many different systems, numbered 1 to 6; number 7 belongs to mycobacteria.

SystemHowExample
Secinner membrane only, unfolded proteinperiplasmic proteins; first step of T2SS and T5SS

T1SSsingle tube in one step, built on an ABC proteinE. coli haemolysin
T2SSsecond step after Sec: from periplasm to outsidecholera toxin
T3SSsyringe: injects proteins into the host cellSalmonella, Shigella, Yersinia
T4SScarries proteins and DNA tooCagA of H. pylori; conjugation
T5SSthe protein makes its own passage through the outer membraneNeisseria IgA protease
T6SSspring-loaded harpoon against other bacteriaVibrio, Pseudomonas
