1 · The old man with swollen ankles: where the fluid comes from

You are the family doctor and you see him at home: eighty-two, thin, hugely swollen ankles and a belly that is starting to grow. Before the drugs comes the right question: where does the fluid leave the vessels, and why does it stop right there? First you see him as you find him, in bed at 45°. Then switch on the cardiovascular counterpart: the circulation appears drawn on his body, with the heart level, the dependent parts where venous blood pools, and the net Starling balance of each capillary bed, including the two portal systems, the liver (vein–capillary–vein) and the kidney (artery–capillary–artery). Tap a bed to see the equation along the capillary, then move albumin and central venous pressure.

The Starling equation

Fluid flow across the capillary wall is Jv = Kf · [(Pc − Pi) − σ(πc − πi)]: hydrostatic pressure in the capillary (Pc) pushes out, tissue pressure (Pi) opposes it; the oncotic pressure of plasma proteins, mainly albumin (πc), pulls back in, tissue oncotic pressure (πi) pulls out; σ says how well the wall holds back proteins (1 = fully, 0 = not at all). Along the capillary Pc falls: at the start the push wins and fluid leaves, at the end, in the classic model, the pull wins and fluid comes back. The lymphatics return what is left. Oedema appears when the venous end filters too and the filtrate exceeds the lymphatics.

The first four are the forces: together they give the net driving force, in mmHg. σ is not a force: it multiplies only the oncotic difference and says how much of it really counts for that wall. Kf is the constant of proportionality that turns force into flow: Jv = Kf × net driving force, in mL/min. A high Kf means a capillary very permeable to water (glomerulus); a low Kf, a poorly permeable one (brain).

Kf is the product of two factors: the capillary surface area available (cm²) and the hydraulic conductance of the wall (Lp, how much fluid crosses each cm² of wall per mmHg: mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹·cm⁻²). So Kf = surface area × Lp. The mmHg is a pressure: 1 mmHg ≈ 133.3 Pa (Pa = N/m²).

Dimensional analysis

Base quantities: mass [M], length [L], time [T].

  • Pressure (force / area): [P] = [M L T⁻²] / [L²] = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²]
  • σ: pure number, [1]; so σ(πc − πi) = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²], like (Pc − Pi)
  • Jv (volume / time): [L³ T⁻¹]
  • Kf = Jv / P = [L³ T⁻¹] / [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] = [M⁻¹ L⁴ T]
  • Surface area: [L²]; Lp = Kf / area = [M⁻¹ L² T], i.e. a velocity [L T⁻¹] divided by a pressure
  • Check: Kf × P = [M⁻¹ L⁴ T] × [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] = [L³ T⁻¹] = Jv ✓

In the widget above the numbers are the net driving force in mmHg, not the flow: to get Jv you would multiply them by that bed's Kf, which the model does not compute.

Indicative values, for teaching. We now know that in many tissues venous reabsorption is modest and that the endothelial glycocalyx shapes the oncotic forces: the logic of the equation stays the same.

2 · The treatment: heart failure, oedema and diuretics

Swollen ankles and a belly full of fluid look the same from outside, but can have very different causes and treatments. Choose one of the seven scenarios, set the treatment and watch three days: weight, oedema, ascites, venous pressure, hormonal alarms, nephron and lab values. Then press "Check the treatment": every choice comes with its reason.

The goal is twofold, and the "Goals" box ticks itself: over 3 days, decongest the patient without harm (weight falling, creatinine, blood pressure and potassium in range); in the long run, protect the heart when needed. The "Patient" view shows the patient and the lab values; the "Physiology" view the circulation, sensors and receptors. In the reduced ejection fraction scenarios, "After 6 months" shows what the four pillars do.

Why the ankles swell

In right heart failure the vis a fronte is lost: the right atrium is already full and at high pressure, and venous blood finds no low-pressure point to flow towards. The body compensates by raising the pressure upstream until the difference is enough to bring blood back to the heart. That high venous pressure reaches the capillaries: fluid leaves into the tissues and does not come back, and the lymphatics, which drain into those very veins, cannot clear it. Gravity takes it to the lowest point: the ankles in someone sitting or standing, the sacrum in someone in bed (sacral oedema). The congested liver lets it seep into the peritoneum, and that is the ascites. In the figure the patient's backrest is at 45°, the position in which the jugular venous pressure is assessed.

Two alarms, and an opposite signal

When the heart pumps poorly, two alarms go off. The sympathetic system (baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch, then the brainstem) makes the heart beat faster and constricts the vessels. The RAAS (renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system) starts in the kidney, which senses low pressure in the afferent arteriole, little salt at the macula densa and the sympathetic drive: angiotensin II constricts vessels, aldosterone holds sodium and water. The stretched atria release ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide), and the ventricles BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide), which call for sodium to be excreted. They are very high, but the kidney wins: what counts is the effective arterial blood volume, how much blood actually reaches the arteries, and in heart failure it is low even when the body is full of water.

Blood pressure is roughly cardiac output × resistance. If output falls, constricting the vessels keeps the pressure up, but raises afterload: the weak heart must push against a higher resistance and pumps even less.

The diuretic removes the water that is there now, but does not prolong life. In heart failure with reduced ejection fraction that is done by the four pillars, which switch off the alarms: sacubitril/valsartan (or an ACE inhibitor or ARB), beta-blocker, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist and SGLT2 inhibitor (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor). With preserved ejection fraction the drug with the best evidence is the SGLT2 inhibitor.

Try it. Goal: reason about where the fluid is and why.
  1. Scenario 1: keep oral furosemide. Why does almost nothing happen?
  2. Scenario 1: switch to 160 mg IV. Why does creatinine rise although the patient is still a little swollen?
  3. Scenario 4: give furosemide. Where does the water it removes come from?
  4. Scenario 5: paracentesis with and without albumin. What changes in sodium and creatinine?
  5. Scenario 7: add metolazone. Look at urine sodium, then at potassium.
