What it is and why it is useful

When two drugs cleared by the same enzyme are taken together, one can change how fast the other is eliminated. In the laboratory that change is measured by two numbers, Km and Vmax. This page shows a way of reading them together: each pair is a point on a plane, and the effect of a drug on the enzyme is the displacement of that point.

What has been shown

That this reading classifies the modulation of an enzyme by direction, intensity and dominant parameter. Checked retrospectively on 25 combinations across three enzyme systems: the direction agreed with the published characterisation in every state, the dominant parameter in nine of thirteen independent cases. Published after peer review in Drug Metabolism and Pharmacokinetics 71 (2026) 101553.

A Km–Vmax pair read as a point

Every point of the plane is a whole Michaelis–Menten curve. Four short sections lead from the curve to the point: the first two recall the classical tools, the third shows the step from curves to points, the fourth reads the plane. All together opens the complete reader at once.

1 · Michaelis–Menten and Lineweaver–Burk

Everything on this page starts from one curve and the straight line it can be turned into. A short reminder of both, then of what a second drug does to them.

The Michaelis–Menten equation

What it is and why it is useful. It says how fast an enzyme works at each substrate concentration. The rate rises, then levels off, and the whole curve is fixed by two numbers: the ceiling it approaches, Vmax, and the concentration at which it reaches half of that ceiling, Km.

v = Vmax · [S]Km + [S]

  • v = reaction rate [N·T−1·M−1] · SI mol·s−1·kg−1; here nmol·min−1·mg−1 of protein
  • [S] = substrate concentration [N·L−3] · SI mol·m−3; here µM (M = mol/L, so 1 µM = 10−3 mol·m−3)
  • Vmax = maximal rate, the ceiling [N·T−1·M−1] · same unit as v
  • Km = Michaelis constant, the concentration that gives half of Vmax [N·L−3] · same unit as [S]
  1. Put the substrate exactly at [S] = Km:  v = Vmax · Km / (Km + Km)
  2. The Km cancels:  v = Vmax / 2
  3. With the untreated enzyme on screen:  v = × / ( + ) = nmol·min−1·mg−1

The Lineweaver–Burk line

What it is and why it is useful. The same equation turned upside down. Plotting 1/v against 1/[S] gives a straight line, and the two numbers can be read where the line meets the axes: 1/Vmax on the vertical axis, −1/Km on the horizontal one.

1v = KmVmax · 1[S] + 1Vmax

  • 1/v = reciprocal rate [N−1·T·M] · here min·mg·nmol−1
  • 1/[S] = reciprocal concentration [N−1·L3] · here µM−1
  • Km/Vmax = slope of the line [L−3·T·M] · here min·mg·mL−1
  1. Take the reciprocal of both sides:  1/v = (Km + [S]) / (Vmax · [S])
  2. Split the fraction in two:  1/v = (Km/Vmax) · 1/[S] + 1/Vmax

2 · Two quantities inside the curve: CLint and Ki

Neither is drawn as a line of its own, yet both can be found on the two plots of section 1. One describes the enzyme, the other the inhibitor.

Intrinsic clearance

What it is and why it is useful. The efficiency of the enzyme when substrate is scarce, which is the usual condition of a drug in the body. It is the one number that predictions of clearance carry forward from the test tube.

CLint = VmaxKm

  • CLint = intrinsic clearance [L3·T−1·M−1] · SI m3·s−1·kg−1; here (nmol·min−1·mg−1)/µM = mL·min−1·mg−1
  • Vmax, Km = as in section 1
  1. When [S] is far below Km, the denominator Km + [S] is almost Km:  v ≈ (Vmax/Km) · [S]
  2. The factor in front of [S] is the intrinsic clearance:  CLint = Vmax/Km
  3. With the untreated enzyme on screen:  CLint = / = mL·min−1·mg−1

Where to find it. On the saturation curve it is the slope with which the curve leaves the origin. On the Lineweaver–Burk plot the slope of the line is Km/Vmax, so CLint is its reciprocal. Both are marked on the plots below.

The inhibition constants

What it is and why it is useful. Ki says how much inhibitor it takes to have an effect: it is the concentration at which half of the free enzyme is held by the inhibitor. A second constant, Ki′, does the same for the enzyme that already holds its substrate. Together they turn an observed change in Km and Vmax into a property of the inhibitor.

3 · Many curves, one point each

An inhibitor tested at four concentrations gives four curves. This section turns each of them into a single point.

The normalised pair

What it is and why it is useful. Curves from different enzymes or laboratories cannot be laid on the same axes, because their units and scales differ. Dividing the two numbers of each curve by those of its own untreated control removes the units. Every curve becomes one point, the control always sits at (1, 1), and curves that could not share a figure can share a plane.

New Which parameter carries the effect

What it is and why it is useful. Two inhibitors can lower the clearance by the same amount and still behave differently in the body. One acts through Km: its effect fades as the substrate rises. The other acts through Vmax: its effect holds at any substrate concentration. The plane tells them apart by combining the direction with the circle.

Part two Open question

Does this reading help predict what happens in the patient?

The clinical measure of an interaction is the AUCR (AUC ratio): the exposure to the victim drug with the inhibitor, divided by its exposure without. Today it is predicted from a single inhibition constant, Ki, measured on one probe reaction. The box below runs that standard calculation, so that what it needs, and where it is weak, can be seen. Whether reading Km and Vmax separately on the plane improves on it is the open question: nothing in this part is a result of the article.

Note · how this is done in practice, and where it is weak The box above is the standard calculation, and every interaction-prediction tool does the same thing. Three weaknesses are built into it.
• One generic Ki, treated as a property of the drug. Interaction software and PBPK libraries carry a single Ki per inhibitor and enzyme. That one number is then applied to every victim drug, at every concentration, in both the liver and the gut.
• It comes from one probe, and no probe represents every mechanism. Greenblatt’s ketoconazole Ki runs from 0.011 to 0.072 µM across five CYP3A marker reactions — and the mechanism itself changes with the probe: mixed on midazolam 1′-hydroxylation, purely competitive on midazolam 4-hydroxylation, the same drug and the same enzyme. A Ki averaged over that, or simply taken from whichever probe a laboratory happened to use, carries none of it forward.
• fm and Fg come from somewhere else again. They are read from clinical studies that have nothing to do with that in vitro experiment, and the same drug often carries several published values: midazolam Fg is reported between 0.39 and 0.57, while the gut term can never exceed 1/Fg. So the intestinal half of the prediction rests on a parameter known to within about 50%.
• Work in progress, not a result. One line of work here is to take the second point seriously: measure one inhibitor across several probe substrates, separate the probe-to-probe disagreement into its Km and Vmax components on this plane, and ask whether it is structured rather than noise — and if it is, what mechanism produces it. The aim is a firmer basis for in vitro–in vivo extrapolation than one averaged Ki. The six reactions behind the ketoconazole · real data button are the start of that set.

What the scheme reads. Polarity comes from the hypotenuse — its tilt is the clearance ratio, so a tilt past 45° is activation. The circle measures how far the state has moved, never in which direction. The triangle flags a pair whose product has drifted, which is where a transcription or unit error usually shows up.

What it does not do. Ki and Ki′ are inverted under one binding site and rapid equilibrium; neither holds for time-dependent inactivation. A ratio pair carries no information about the quality of the fit it came from, so a wide confidence interval on Vmax passes through this tool silently.
