Two constants bridge the two forms:

  1. Henry: from pressure to concentration. For a dissolved gas C = α · P, so ΔC = α · ΔP. Solubility α has dimensions [N L⁻³] / [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] = [N M⁻¹ L⁻² T²].
  2. Molar volume: from moles to volume. V̇ = (J · A) · Vm, with Vm ≈ 22.4 L·mol⁻¹ at STPD (0 °C, 1 atm, dry gas), dimensions [L³ N⁻¹].

Substituting into form A: V̇ = Vm · D · A · α · ΔP / T = (D · α · Vm) · A · ΔP / T. The D of the gas form is the D of the general form multiplied by α and by Vm: that is where the extra dimensions go.

Check: [L² T⁻¹] × [N M⁻¹ L⁻² T²] × [L³ N⁻¹] = [M⁻¹ L³ T] = Dgas ✓.

The law is the same: only the quantities measured change, pressure instead of concentration and volume instead of moles. That is why the gas D is no longer a plain diffusivity: it also depends on solubility, which is why CO₂ diffuses about 20 times better than O₂.

In fish: same formula, another strategy

Fish follow the same Fick's law, but with a much harder medium: water. On the terms of the formula most fish do worse than us: less area per kilo and a thicker barrier. Only the most active ones, such as tunas, reach our values. What saves them is how they move the water.

Indicative values. A sluggish fish, with little area and a 5–6 µm barrier, has a diffusing capacity about 140 times lower than a tuna of the same size (Wegner).

Why water is hard. Compared with air it holds about 30 times less O₂, so about 30 times more must be moved to get the same oxygen; O₂ diffuses in it about 10,000 times more slowly; and it is about 800 times denser and 50 times more viscous, so moving it costs a lot of energy.

The solution: countercurrent flow. In the lamellae water and blood flow in opposite directions. Blood about to leave, already nearly saturated, meets the water that has just come in, the richest in O₂; blood that has just arrived, poor in O₂, meets the water about to leave, already depleted but still richer than it. So P1 − P2 stays positive along the whole lamella and exchange does not stop halfway. In our lung, by contrast, air goes in and out by the same route and mixes with the old air: that is why we extract much less.

Why tuna suffocate if they stop swimming

  1. Most fish pump water themselves. They open and close the mouth and opercula and push water over the gills even when still: the buccal-opercular pump.
  2. Tuna have lost this pump. Their gills are packed with lamellae, partly fused together to withstand the strong flow of fast swimming: they offer a lot of resistance to water. The pump could not push enough for their very high O₂ consumption. So the tuna swims with its mouth open and lets speed drive the water through: ram ventilation. The cost of breathing moves to the swimming muscles, which it uses anyway.
  3. If it stops, P1 − P2 drops to zero. The water between the lamellae is no longer renewed. Blood keeps taking O₂ from that thin film until its PO₂ falls to that of the blood: the gradient vanishes, and with it V̇O₂, while the oxygen demand stays very high. The tuna becomes hypoxic and suffocates.

It is the same term that falls in us with hypoventilation: if the medium next to the barrier is not renewed, perfect area and thickness are no use. Some sharks too, such as the mako and the great white, breathe only by swimming; others, such as the tiger shark, can switch between pumping and swimming depending on speed.

3 · The Nernst potential

EX = (RT / zF) · ln([X]out / [X]in)

Picture a membrane that lets through a single ion, K⁺, more concentrated inside than outside. K⁺ leaves down its concentration gradient and takes its positive charge with it: the inside becomes negative. That negativity pulls it back. When the chemical push outwards and the electrical pull inwards balance, net flux stops: K⁺ keeps crossing in both directions, but on average it no longer moves. That voltage is the ion's Nernst equilibrium (or reversal) potential. Only the ratio of the concentrations matters, not their absolute values nor the permeability of the membrane.

Very few ions are needed. A spherical cell 50 µm across has a membrane capacitance of about 78.5 pF (1 µF per cm²). Charging it to −70 mV takes 5.5 × 10⁻¹² C, about 34 million K⁺ ions, out of about 5.5 × 10¹² K⁺ ions in the cell: the concentration changes by 0.0006 %. The potential arises without any measurable change in concentration.

From Nernst to the resting potential. Nernst holds for one ion. The real membrane is permeable to several ions, and the resting potential settles near the equilibrium potential of the ion it is most permeable to: at rest K⁺, through leak channels. Weighing all ions together by their permeability takes the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation. The difference Vm − EX is the driving force on the ion: it says which way it goes and how strongly.

Clinically. In hyperkalaemia outside K⁺ rises, for example from 4 to 7 mM: EK moves from −95 to about −80 mV, cells depolarise and heart and muscle become unstable (arrhythmias). Try it in the figure with the “K⁺ · hyperkalaemia” example.
